ClientScore — the pre-pitch reputation brief on every UK client, built from real freelancer payment data.

A freelancer's credit bureau for clients — tells you, before you pitch, whether the agency pays on time, how it treats scope changes, and what similar projects actually paid.

Creative freelancers lose thousands of pounds a year pitching to clients who pay late, dispute scope, or vanish mid-project. The information about how a client treats freelancers is out there — buried in late invoices, scope-creep disputes, and forum complaints — but no one has aggregated it into something queryable. ClientScore builds the first UK dataset of structured client-payment behaviour as reported by the freelancers who have actually worked with them, then sells a one-page brief the freelancer reads before sending a pitch: payment days, dispute rate, repeat-hire pattern, and what similar projects in the same sector closed at.

The model is borrowed directly from consumer credit bureaux — Experian made a business out of pooling payment data on individuals and selling it to lenders who needed it before extending credit. Here the 'lender' is the freelancer deciding whether to take on a new client, the 'borrower' is the hiring company, and the 'payment data' is the late-invoice evidence that already exists in thousands of freelance inboxes across the UK.

Revenue: £9 a month for unlimited searches, or £3 for a single pre-pitch brief. Growth comes from two compounding channels. First, SEO for queries like '[agency name] freelancer reviews' and '[agency name] payment reputation' — the exact searches a freelancer does before taking a gig. Second, partnerships with UK invoicing platforms (Bonsai, AND CO, FreshBooks) where freelancers can opt to share anonymised payment data in exchange for a permanent badge on their profile that says 'Verified Payer'. Retention compounds with each new report — once a freelancer has searched three clients and seen the depth of the dataset, a competing tool with fewer data points cannot tempt them across.

This borrows the bureau model and applies it to a market that has zero structured intelligence today: the freelance-to-client payment economy.

UK creative freelancers — designers, copywriters, developers, brand consultants and photographers earning £30k to £80k a year, currently pitching two to five new clients per quarter and burning unpaid hours on the wrong ones.

Three signals converged in 2024 and 2025. The UK Late Payment Act reforms put new statutory teeth behind late-payment claims, making payment behaviour a hotter topic than it has been in a decade. Companies House filings are now fully open and machine-readable, so the company-side context is free to pull. And large language models make it cheap to turn unstructured freelancer chatter — Reddit threads, design Slack complaints, public LinkedIn posts — into structured signals at scale. The freelance share of the UK workforce has also grown by roughly 40% since 2020, which is finally enough volume for a national dataset to feel credible.
