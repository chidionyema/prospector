CutList — turns a furniture designer's sketch into a cutting plan the workshop will actually quote.

A free tool for freelance furniture and interior designers that converts a rough drawing into a panel cutting list, a costed material sheet, and a file any UK CNC shop can machine from.

A freelance furniture designer draws a piece in SketchUp, Fusion or on paper. To get it made, they must hand a UK workshop something the workshop can actually price: a panel-by-panel cutting list, board thicknesses, edge banding lengths, grain direction, and hardware. Today that translation happens by email, over days, and the quote comes back wrong because the workshop guessed.

CutList is a browser tool the designer uses free. Upload a model or draw the carcass. It outputs the cutting list, the nesting layout on standard UK board sizes (2440x1220mm MDF, 18mm birch ply), the offcut waste percentage, and a costed sheet at live trade prices. Free forever for the designer, because the designer is not the payer.

The growth motion is the file itself. Every cutting list the designer sends carries a link. The workshop opens it, sees a machine-ready DXF and a nest they would otherwise spend forty minutes producing, and gets asked to claim their shop. Claimed shops pay from £59/month to receive jobs pre-nested to their own machine bed, their own kerf, their own board stock, and to quote back inside the tool. The designer's file is the salesperson.

The thing that compounds is the shop parameter library. Every claimed workshop's real bed size, spoilboard, tooling, minimum part size, edge banding stock, and actual quoted price per square metre against a known geometry. After a few thousand jobs, CutList can tell a designer in Bristol what their piece will cost before any human quotes it, and can route the job to the three shops whose machines fit it. Neither the designer's CAD tool nor a board merchant can assemble that: the price-versus-geometry pairs only exist where quotes cross between two parties, and CutList sits in the middle of that crossing by owning the file format both sides use.

This was built by ignoring how making furniture is organised today and asking the raw question: what is the smallest object that must pass from the person who designs to the person who cuts, and who is hurt when it is wrong?

UK specificity: board sizes and trade pricing follow UK merchants; timber sourced for commercial furniture falls under the UK Timber Regulation enforced by the Office for Product Safety and Standards, and CutList records the species and supplier declaration on each list, which a designer selling to a contract client is asked to evidence.

UK CNC and panel-processing workshops with 2 to 15 staff — the joinery shops in trading estates outside Manchester, Leeds and Bristol that currently win work by phone. They pay £59 to £199 a month to be a machine-ready destination for designers' files and to quote inside the tool. The freelance designer never pays.

Affordable flatbed CNC routers have put nesting capability into small UK joinery shops that had only a panel saw five years ago, so a machine-ready DXF is now usable by hundreds of shops rather than a handful. At the same time freelance furniture and interiors work has shifted to remote clients, so the designer rarely stands in the workshop to explain the drawing. Timber due-diligence duties under the UK Timber Regulation, enforced by the Office for Product Safety and Standards, also mean species and supplier now have to be recorded rather than assumed.
