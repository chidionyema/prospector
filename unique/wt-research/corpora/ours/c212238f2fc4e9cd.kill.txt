Model release notes for AI startups selling to banks.

A tool for AI startups selling to UK banks and insurers that turns every model or prompt change into the written record the buyer's model risk team demands before it goes live.

Here is the plain version. If you sell an AI product to a UK bank, building society or insurer, your buyer has an internal team whose job is to sign off every model they rely on. Every time you retrain, swap a foundation model, or change a system prompt, that team wants to know what changed and what it did to accuracy. Today a founder answers this in email threads and hand-written Word documents, weeks after the change shipped. This tool sits on the startup's own deployment pipeline and writes that record automatically, in the format the buyer's committee already uses.

The borrowed idea comes from aviation. Aircraft parts carry a certificate that travels with the component and records every modification and who authorised it, so an airline can accept the part without re-testing it. Nobody does this for models sold into regulated finance. The startup's model gets a travelling record instead: version, training data cut-off, eval scores before and after, who approved it, and what the change did to the outcomes the bank cares about.

Why the pain is real and already paid for. The Bank of England and the Prudential Regulation Authority set out model risk management expectations for banks in supervisory statement SS1/23, which took effect on 17 May 2024. It puts responsibility on the firm for models it buys in from third parties, and expects them to be identified, documented and reviewed. Separately, the FCA and PRA rules on operational resilience and outsourcing (SS2/21) mean a bank must be able to explain a supplier's material changes. The bank cannot delegate that duty, so the burden lands on the small vendor as a questionnaire.

The buyer is the founder or head of engineering at a UK AI startup with a signed or nearly-signed deal with a name like Lloyds, Nationwide, Aviva or a mid-tier building society. The trigger recurs on every model change, which for a live AI product is monthly or weekly. The deliverable is a signed change record plus a quarterly pack the startup hands to the bank's model risk committee.

Why it holds. Each record is only meaningful next to the previous one. After a year, the startup has a chain of evidence about its own model behaviour that exists nowhere else, and that a new bank's due diligence team will ask to see. Ripping the tool out means losing the chain and starting the audit history from zero, mid-sale. That is the switching cost.

Growth comes from the bank, not from cold outreach. Every bank that accepts a vendor's pack has ten more AI vendors in its pipeline facing the same questionnaire, and vendors talk. The startup sells self-serve at roughly £600 a month, rising with the number of models under record.
