ProofShift — turns a gig worker's scattered app earnings into one income statement a landlord or lender will actually accept.

A fixed-fee service for gig workers that pulls their pay from every platform they drive or deliver for and turns it into one standard income certificate letting agents and lenders recognise.

This is built on the same idea as the certificate a small accountancy firm writes for a self-employed plumber applying for a mortgage: a short, signed document that translates messy income into a form a bank's checklist accepts. ProofShift does the same job for a 21-year-old who drives for Uber three nights a week, delivers for Deliveroo at lunchtime, and picks up Amazon Flex blocks at weekends.

The pain is specific and daily. A gig worker trying to rent a flat, get a phone contract, or finance a car hits an automated affordability check built around a single steady payslip. Referencing services like Homelet and Goodlord, which most UK letting agents use, ask for proof of income in a format gig income never arrives in — one PDF per platform, different date ranges, gross figures mixed with net, no employer letterhead. The applicant is rejected or told to find a guarantor, not because they don't earn enough, but because nobody has translated what they earn into the shape the checker wants.

ProofShift asks the worker to connect their bank account through a licensed open banking provider (the same kind of connection TrueLayer or Plaid UK already provide to regulated fintechs) and upload or link each platform's own earnings export. A template engine reconciles the different reporting periods and definitions into one certificate, a human reviewer signs off anything unusual, and the worker gets a PDF with a verification code the letting agent or lender can check is genuine. The worker pays a flat fee, roughly £15–£25, each time they need a certificate — moving flat, changing car finance, opening a new contract.

The FCA's Consumer Duty rules, in force since July 2023, put pressure on lenders and referencing firms to treat customers with non-standard income fairly rather than auto-declining them, which gives ProofShift's certificate somewhere to land: firms have a reason to accept a well-evidenced third-party statement instead of guessing. What keeps a bank or the platforms themselves from just doing this is that no single platform has an incentive to reconcile a driver's income from its competitors — Uber's export only covers Uber. Over time ProofShift builds a growing record of which format each lender, letting agent chain and mobile network will actually accept without further questions, and a track record of verified certificates that makes fraud checks faster — a dataset a new entrant starting from zero doesn't have.
