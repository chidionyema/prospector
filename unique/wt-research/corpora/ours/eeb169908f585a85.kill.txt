Proof Card, the paper that ships with your art print.

A printed, numbered certificate and archive kit that freelance illustrators and photographers sell alongside each print, so buyers get proof of authorship and the seller gets a repeat purchase.

This is a physical product sold by post. You buy a pack: numbered certificate cards printed on cotton stock, tamper-evident foil seals, a signing pen that survives the paper, and a small logbook. The illustrator writes the edition number, signs it, seals it, and posts it with the print.

Start from the raw pain rather than how the trade solves it today. An illustrator selling a £180 limited edition on Etsy or at an art fair has nothing to hand the buyer that says the print is genuinely one of fifty. Galleries have had certificates for a century. A solo seller has a receipt from a payment app.

The pain is not sentimental. It is resale and it is copyright. When a buyer resells, the print with no paperwork sells for less. When someone copies the work, the artist needs to show a dated record of the edition. The UK has no artist registry that does this for people selling from a kitchen table.

The kit is designed around one UK-specific fact. Under the Artist's Resale Right Regulations 2006, an artist gets a royalty when a qualifying work is resold through an auction house or dealer above €1,000. The collecting society DACS pays those royalties, but only if the work can be traced to the artist. A numbered, signed certificate is exactly the trace that makes a later claim possible. The kit ships with a plain-English page explaining who DACS are, how to register, and what the certificate is for. Almost no illustrator selling at Bristol Comic Expo knows this exists.

Why it holds. The certificate design and numbering system belong to the seller of the kit, and every artist who adopts it is now committed: their edition 3/50 certificates must match their edition 4/50 certificates for years, or the set looks broken. That is real switching cost, built by the buyer's own back catalogue rather than by anything technical. A rival printer can sell blank cards. They cannot make an artist's existing forty certificates match a new supplier's stock.

Running it solo is realistic. One person orders cotton card and foil seals from a UK trade printer in runs of a few thousand, packs kits at home, and posts them second class in a stiffened envelope. Distribution is free and owned: a stall table at UK comic and art fairs where the exact buyers stand for two days, plus the artist's own newsletter and the sample certificate that every one of their customers receives in the post. Each sold print is a small advert carrying the maker's mark.

Pricing: around £39 for a starter pack of 50 certificates and seals, £59 for 150, and a personalised version at £95 where the artist's name and a permanent studio mark are printed into the card. The personalised version is where the repeat revenue sits, because it cannot be substituted once an edition is running.

Freelance illustrators, printmakers and photographers in the UK who sell limited-edition prints priced £60 and up. They sell at comic and art fairs, on Etsy, and from their own sites. They already spend on good paper, giclée printing and postal tubes, so a £39 pack that raises what a print is worth is an easy yes.

Two things changed. Print-on-demand made it trivially cheap to run a small edition, so the market is flooded and buyers now want to know which prints are genuinely limited. Meanwhile image-generating software has made 'is this really yours?' a live question at every art fair table in the country. Sellers are being asked, in person, to prove authorship. There is nothing in their hand to answer with.
