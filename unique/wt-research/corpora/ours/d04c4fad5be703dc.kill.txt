Deposit Rescue — the platform where a stalled house-sale chain buys out the person who has to drop out.

When a property chain collapses, this platform lets the remaining buyers and sellers pay the person who must pull out, so the chain survives and nobody restarts from scratch.

A house-sale chain in England and Wales is a line of linked deals that all complete on the same day. Because nothing is legally binding until contracts are exchanged, one person dropping out kills every deal in the line. Around a quarter to a third of agreed sales fall through before completion, and each collapse burns the survey, conveyancing and mortgage-arrangement money everyone already spent.

Here is the plain version. When a chain wobbles, the person about to drop out is usually not being difficult. They have a specific, priceable problem: their own buyer vanished, their mortgage offer expired, or their new-build completion slipped by six weeks. Deposit Rescue is a marketplace where the other people in that chain club together and pay that person a sum to stay in, or to step aside cleanly and be replaced. The platform prices the offer, holds the money, and releases it only when contracts are exchanged.

The money side is the actual product. Each participant enters what they have already spent and what a collapse would cost them: sunk fees, a lost mortgage rate, a rental gap, a second stamp duty bill. The platform totals that across the chain and shows everyone the number they are collectively about to set on fire. Against that number, a £4,000 payment to bridge one person's six-week gap is obviously cheap. Most people never see this arithmetic because nobody in a chain is allowed to see anyone else's position. Estate agents hold the fragments and have no duty, and no mechanism, to pool them.

This takes the idea behind an airline paying volunteers to give up a seat on an overbooked flight, and points it at property. The airline discovered that a cash offer at the gate is far cheaper than the chaos of a forced bump. A chain is the same shape: one person's exit destroys value for four other households, so paying them is rational, and nobody has ever built the place to do it.

The twist is what happens as the rules get harsher, not softer. Government has consulted repeatedly on reservation agreements and binding contracts to cut fall-throughs, and the Home Buying and Selling Group keeps pushing the same direction. Every step towards binding commitments makes stepping out expensive, which makes the price of a buy-out more visible and more worth paying. A regulator can mandate that people commit. It cannot run a commercial market that sets the price of releasing one of them, and it cannot take one side's part in a private negotiation. That gap only widens as the rules tighten.

The payment is a percentage of each rescue, charged to the parties who benefit, typically 10% of the sum transferred, with a floor of around £600. On a typical £4,000 bridge in a four-party chain, that is a few hundred pounds each against the several thousand each stood to lose.

What compounds is the settlement data. Every completed rescue records the chain's shape, the reason for the wobble, what was offered, what was refused, and what finally closed it. Within a few thousand deals the platform can quote, on day one of a wobble, the number that historically settles this exact situation in this exact region. Nobody else holds that. Estate agents see one link, conveyancers see one file, and the Land Registry sees only completions, never the near-deaths. That priced record of how much it costs to save a chain is the asset, and no rival can buy it.

Distribution runs through conveyancers and mortgage brokers, who lose their fee when a chain dies and have every reason to hand the client a way to save it. A referral fee to them makes it a repeatable channel rather than one-by-one selling.

Buyers and sellers aged 35-55 in chains of three or more, moving in the £300,000 to £700,000 band, who have already spent £2,000 to £5,000 on searches, surveys and legal work and hold a mortgage offer with a clock on it. The paying moment is the fortnight after someone announces they are pulling out. Conveyancing firms and mortgage brokers introduce the clients and take a referral fee, because a collapsed chain costs them their own completion fee.

Roughly a quarter to a third of agreed sales in England and Wales still collapse before exchange, and the government has consulted on reservation agreements and binding contracts specifically to reduce that. Mortgage offers now commonly run three to six months, so a delayed link in a chain pushes buyers past their rate expiry into a materially worse deal, which puts a hard, calculable price on a few weeks of slippage for the first time.
