SponsorGuard — cash cover for the day the Home Office suspends your visa sponsor licence.

SponsorGuard pays out when the Home Office suspends a UK startup's sponsor licence, covering relocation, replacement hiring and lost contracts, priced from audit data no insurer currently holds.

Any UK company that sponsors overseas staff under a Skilled Worker visa holds a licence from the Home Office, and that licence can be suspended or revoked for even a small paperwork slip — a missed right-to-work check, a late change-of-circumstances report, an HR system that doesn't flag an employee's changed job title. When that happens, every sponsored worker's visa is put at risk within days, and a fast-growing startup can lose a large share of its engineering team almost overnight while it scrambles to find a new sponsor or watches people leave the country.

SponsorGuard is a bond a startup buys alongside its sponsor licence renewal. If the Home Office suspends or revokes the licence, it pays out cash straight away — enough to cover emergency relocation costs for affected staff, agency fees to backfill roles fast, and penalty clauses in client contracts that depend on named engineers being available. It's written as a delegated-authority policy on Lloyd's or UK insurer paper, so the founders don't need their own insurance licence to start selling it — the same route firms like Zego and Marshmallow used to get underwriting off the ground quickly.

The reason this holds is the data. Home Office compliance visit outcomes, common revocation triggers, and typical time-to-resolution aren't published anywhere an actuary can price today, so mainstream commercial insurers won't touch this risk. Every policy SponsorGuard writes and every claim it pays adds real outcome data most insurers structurally don't have, sharpening its pricing edge with each renewal cycle. And the Home Office itself can never sell this cover — it is the very body deciding whether to revoke the licence, so it has no way to insure a company against its own enforcement action.

UK scale-ups, typically Series A to C with 50 to 500 staff, that hold a Home Office Skilled Worker sponsor licence and sponsor five or more overseas employees — bought by the founder or Head of People who signs the licence renewal and knows what losing it would cost the business.

Since the Skilled Worker minimum salary threshold rose to £38,700 in April 2024 and the Immigration Salary List replaced the old shortage occupation list, Home Office compliance visits and licence actions have stepped up sharply, and sponsor licence holders are named on a public Home Office register the moment they're suspended. Startups that spent the last few years hiring overseas engineers to plug the UK's tech skills gap now carry a concentrated, largely uninsured risk they didn't have before.

The passages show revocations more than doubling year-on-year (937 to 1,948; 1,560 in 2024-25) alongside explicit Home Office framing of this as deliberate 'real action to secure our border' — meaning revocation risk is a rapidly rising, actively-enforced trend, not a stable or fading one, which cuts against pricing a durable, steady-state peril from clean actuarial trend data. Rising, policy-driven enforcement volatility undermines rather than supports the claim that the underlying risk data is stable enough to be a durable proprietary pricing edge.
