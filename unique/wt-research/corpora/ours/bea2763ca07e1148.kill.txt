Second Charge Sweep — the fixed-fee check that finds the old loan still secretly attached to your house.

A fixed-fee service for homeowners aged 35-55 remortgaging or selling, that finds and clears forgotten charges and restrictions stuck on their Land Registry title.

You are remortgaging, or selling, and your solicitor tells you three weeks before completion that something old is registered against your house. It might be a second charge from a loan settled in 2011. It might be a restriction from a building society that no longer exists, a defunct doorstep lender, or a Green Deal charge from an insulation scheme. The lender who put it there is gone, absorbed, or renamed twice. Now the sale is stalling and everyone is charging by the hour.

This is a flat-fee job: £250 to pull the official copy of your title from HM Land Registry, read every entry in the Charges Register, and tell you plainly what is on there and what it will take to remove. Then £400 to £900 to actually do the removals — tracing who now holds the debt, chasing the discharge form (DS1 or DS3), or building the evidence pack for a Form RX3 or CN1 application when nobody can be found at all.

The interesting part is that this gets more valuable the harder the Land Registry works. Their backlog on complex applications has run into many months, and every push toward faster digital conveyancing raises the cost of a title that will not go through clean. The Registry publishes excellent free guidance and free forms. What it structurally cannot do is take your side. It is the neutral body deciding your application — it cannot chase a lender on your behalf, cannot argue that the evidence is sufficient, and cannot tell you which of three routes to a removal will actually be accepted. That adversarial position is the whole business, and no registry can occupy it.

The compounding asset is a private ledger of who now holds the paper for defunct UK lenders. Every case adds a row: which successor entity, which department, which email address actually replies, how many days it took, which wording got the discharge signed. Northern Rock, Bradford & Bingley, GE Money, Welcome Finance, the small building societies swallowed in mergers — that map exists nowhere as a single document. After a hundred cases you can answer in ten minutes what takes a solicitor three weeks of letters, and you charge a fixed fee because you already know the answer.

One person runs this from a laptop. Land Registry searches cost a few pounds each. The work is reading, tracing, and drafting. No licence is needed to prepare and lodge Land Registry applications on your own behalf or to assist with them, and the moment a case turns into a genuine legal dispute you hand it to a solicitor and take the referral.

Distribution is owned and free. You publish the successor map itself — one page per dead lender, naming who holds it now and what the discharge process is. People searching the exact name of a lender that no longer exists find you, because nobody else has written that page.

Homeowners in their late thirties to mid fifties who are remortgaging to a better rate, downsizing, or selling to fund a move, and whose sale has stalled on an old entry in the title. Also independent conveyancing solicitors and licensed conveyancers in small firms, who hit these cases a few times a month and would rather pay a fixed fee to have the tracing done than burn a paralegal's week on it.

Remortgaging volumes are high as the cheap fixed-rate deals taken before 2022 keep expiring, so a large cohort of owners are having their titles examined for the first time in years. HM Land Registry's own published processing times show complex applications taking many months, which turns a stale charge from paperwork into a chain-collapsing deadline. Two decades of secured-lender failures and mergers — the 2008 casualties, the doorstep and subprime lenders wound down since, the Green Deal charges registered from 2013 — have left a large stock of titles with entries whose owner is not obvious from the register itself.
