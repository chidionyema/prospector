Cloud region carbon disclosure audits for SaaS ops leads.

A subscription intelligence report telling UK software firms which of their cloud regions and vendors will fail the emissions figures they must publish, before the auditor asks.

UK companies over a certain size must publish their energy use and carbon emissions in their annual accounts. The rules are called Streamlined Energy and Carbon Reporting, and they sit in The Companies (Directors' Report) and Limited Liability Partnerships (Energy and Carbon Report) Regulations 2018. A software company's biggest chunk is cloud computing, and nobody inside the firm knows what it actually is.

The operations manager gets handed this every year. They ask AWS, Azure and Google for numbers. What comes back is a vendor dashboard built to flatter the vendor: it uses the market-based method, which lets a provider net off renewable energy certificates bought anywhere. UK reporting wants the location-based figure too, which reflects the actual grid the servers sit on. Those two numbers can differ by a factor of ten for the same workload.

The product is a quarterly report per customer. It takes their cloud bill line items, which they already have, and reprices the compute against published grid carbon intensity for the country each region physically sits in. National Grid ESO publishes half-hourly carbon intensity for Great Britain, free and open. The equivalent numbers for Ireland, Sweden, Virginia and Frankfurt are public too. The report says: this workload in eu-west-1 carries this many tonnes on the honest measure, moving it to London changes it by this much, and here is the sentence your directors' report needs.

The thing that compounds is the archive. Every customer report adds a dated observation of what a specific instance type in a specific region actually emitted at a specific hour. After two years the operator holds a time series of real UK workload emissions that no vendor publishes, because vendors publish annual averages and only for their own estate. That archive lets the operator answer the question an auditor asks and a dashboard cannot: was this number defensible on the day it was measured.

This is the inverting angle at work. The value grows as reporting rules tighten and as the Financial Reporting Council pushes harder on the quality of these disclosures. A regulator can publish the rule and even a free calculator. It cannot take the reporting company's side, cannot argue with a cloud vendor's methodology on a customer's behalf, and cannot hold a private archive of one firm's historic workload placement.

One person runs this. Cloud bills arrive as files, grid data arrives by open feed, the analysis is code, and the writing is a template plus judgment. Distribution is the operations manager's own world: the report itself is the marketing, published in redacted form as a quarterly UK cloud emissions note that finance and ops people forward to each other.

Operations managers and heads of infrastructure at UK software and data companies with 250-plus staff, who must sign off the energy and carbon section of the annual accounts and currently guess at the cloud figure. Also the finance director who signs the directors' report.

Streamlined Energy and Carbon Reporting has applied to large UK companies for annual periods since April 2019, so most software firms are now several cycles in and the auditor questions have started. The UK is also building its own sustainability reporting standards on top of the international ones, which raises the bar on how emissions figures are evidenced. Meanwhile cloud spend at these firms keeps growing, so the cloud share of the disclosure keeps growing with it. Grid carbon intensity data is now published openly and at half-hourly resolution, which makes honest recalculation possible for the first time without buying a data licence.
