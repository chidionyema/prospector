Cure Kit — the two-part epoxy and pigment kit that lets a freelance designer sell resin river tables without a workshop.

A pre-measured resin, hardener and pigment kit posted to freelance makers, matched to one exact mould size, so the pour works first time.

This borrows the way meal-kit companies pre-portion every ingredient so a nervous cook cannot get the ratio wrong, and applies it to casting resin.

Freelance designers, illustrators and product people increasingly sell physical objects to smooth out lumpy client income. Casting resin is the obvious route: high ticket, low tooling. The problem is that deep-pour epoxy fails hard. Mix the wrong ratio by a few percent and the piece stays tacky forever. Pour deeper than the product allows and it overheats, yellows or cracks. The buyer loses £80 of material and a week.

The product is a boxed kit for one specific outcome, not a generic tin of resin. Example: 'A2 river table insert, 40mm deep, walnut edge'. Inside are resin and hardener already weighed to the gram for that exact volume, one sachet of pigment, mixing sticks, a temperature strip, and a single card with pour times for a UK room at 18-22°C. Sold at around £95-£140 a kit, direct from your own site, with a repeat pigment refill at £18.

The durable part is not the chemistry, which anyone can buy. It is the pour data. Every kit ships with a QR code to a two-minute outcome form: room temperature, mould, result, photo. After a few hundred kits you know which pigment and depth combinations fail in a cold UK garage in February, and you re-cut the ratios and the timing card for the next batch. Nobody selling resin by the litre collects that, because they never know what shape the customer poured.

Under UK rules, epoxy resins and hardeners are classified and must carry proper labelling under the GB CLP Regulation, with a safety data sheet supplied to the buyer. Amine hardeners are skin sensitisers. You are repacking, not manufacturing, but you still sit in the supply chain, so you take the supplier's safety data sheet, relabel each container correctly, and include it in the box. HSE publishes the GB CLP guidance and the labelling rules are on legislation.gov.uk. That paperwork is genuinely off-putting, which is exactly why the crowded resin market is full of unlabelled eBay repackers and short of anyone doing it properly.

Distribution is owned: short-form video of pours that fail and pours that work, which is already the single most-watched craft format, plus the mailing list from kit registrations.

One person runs it. You buy resin in 20-litre drums, decant on a bench with a set of scales, print labels, and post via a courier. Fifty kits a week is a bench day.

Deep-pour epoxy furniture is a sustained seller on UK Etsy and Instagram, not a spike, and hardwood offcut prices make a river table one of the few high-margin things a solo maker can produce at home. At the same time, GB CLP labelling duties after the UK left the EU chemical system mean casual repackers are increasingly non-compliant, which opens room for a properly labelled kit.
