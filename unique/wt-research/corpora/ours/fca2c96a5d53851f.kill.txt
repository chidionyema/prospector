DoorMap, floor plans that pass Building Regs Part M.

A drawing tool for people opening a small venue, clinic or shop, which checks every door, ramp and loo against the access rules before a building control officer sees it.

Someone starting a physical business signs a lease, pays a designer, then finds out months later that the accessible loo is 100mm too narrow. The fix is structural and expensive. The rules that decide this are Approved Document M of the Building Regulations, plus BS 8300-2:2018 for the detail, plus the duty to make reasonable adjustments under the Equality Act 2010.

DoorMap is a browser drawing tool a non-architect can use. You trace your unit, drop in doors, counters, ramps, WCs and seating. As you draw, it flags the numbers that matter: 750mm clear door width, 1500mm turning circle, 1:20 ramp gradient, threshold heights, the location of the emergency alarm pull cord in the accessible WC. It exports a marked-up plan a building control body or a licensing officer will accept as a starting point, and a snag list ranked by what costs most to fix later.

The angle is borrowed from aviation. Pilots do not remember rules; they run a checklist that a machine walks them through, item by item, before the aircraft moves. Nobody applies that to a first-time cafe owner drawing a floor plan, even though the cost of missing an item is comparable in their world.

The part that compounds is what happens after submission. Every plan run through DoorMap gets an outcome: approved, queried, or rejected, and by which of the 400-odd approved inspectors and local authority building control teams in England and Wales. Within a year the tool can tell a founder in Leeds which specific interpretations their own inspector has queried before. No published source contains that. It comes only from watching thousands of real submissions land.

Growth comes from the forcing function rather than from selling. You cannot open the doors without this being right. Commercial letting agents, shopfitters and the RIBA-registered practices that turn down small jobs all have reason to hand the tool over, because it removes the unpaid hand-holding they currently do for free.

Founders fitting out their first physical site: independent coffee shops, private dental and physiotherapy clinics, nurseries, barbers, small gyms, taprooms and pet groomers. They pay a few hundred pounds per site, against a shopfitting budget that typically runs from £30,000 upward, and a rework bill that starts at several thousand.

Approved Document M has been in force for years, but two things changed. The Building Safety Act 2022 gave the new Building Safety Regulator real teeth and made building control bodies register and account for their decisions, so inspectors are noticeably less willing to wave things through. And accessibility claims under the Equality Act 2010 against small venues have become a routine cost of trading, with solicitors sending pre-action letters over step-free access. The two pressures land on the same person: whoever signed the lease.
