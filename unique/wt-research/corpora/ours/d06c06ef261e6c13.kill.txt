Deployment sign-offs for UK AI vendors.

Signed evidence packs for each stage of a customer's AI deployment, modelled on building control sign-off, so enterprise buyers can show their auditor the work was properly staged.

UK AI startups selling into FCA-regulated firms are being asked for per-deployment evidence by their enterprise buyers. The buyer is no longer satisfied with an annual SOC 2 report and a SIG-Lite questionnaire — the procurement team wants to see, for THIS customer's deployment, that data access was set up correctly, that the model version is locked, that monitoring is wired, and that rollback was rehearsed. The enterprise buyer's regulator (FCA under SMCR, ICO under the AI Auditing Framework) is the audience for that evidence. The work is the bottleneck: AI startups stall revenue because their customer success engineers spend weeks assembling PDFs after every go-live, and the deal slips when the pack is late.

This borrows the way building control inspectors sign off each stage of a UK construction project (a statutory pattern under the Building Regulations 2010 and the Building Safety Act 2022), and applies the same stage-gate inspection pattern to AI deployments. The deliverable is a per-deployment logbook in three stages: pre-deployment (data access, model lock, rollback plan), go-live (monitoring hooks, customer training, runbook), and in-life (version control, drift alerts, audit trail). Each stage is signed off by an inspector — an automated audit pipeline for the mechanical checks, and a registered human reviewer for the edge cases — and the customer receives a signed certificate per stage that their procurement team can hand to the regulator.

The wedge is the inspection protocol itself: the auditor-friendly evidence schema, the automated checks against the customer's deployment configuration, and the integration into the vendor's CI/CD. An incumbent cannot capture this for three structural reasons. First, SOC 2 audit firms (Drata, Vanta, Tugboat Logic) sell organisational-level compliance refreshed annually, not per-deployment stage sign-offs, and they have no concept of a registered independent inspector. Second, the Big Four (PwC, EY, Deloitte, KPMG) could add a per-deployment service but their unit economics (consultant hours) make a £15k–£30k fee per deployment impossible for an AI startup whose customer pays them a six-figure ACV. Third, only an independent approved inspector sits in the right place: the vendor cannot self-sign their own deployment without the regulator discounting it, and the buyer cannot sign on their own behalf. That role does not yet exist for AI, and that is what this tool creates.

The Customer Success Lead or VP Sales at a UK AI startup (typically 10–80 people, ARR £1m–£10m) selling to FCA-regulated enterprise buyers. They pay because each new deployment currently costs them 2–4 engineer-weeks of evidence assembly, and the deal slips when evidence is late.

The FCA's Consumer Duty (in force from July 2023 for new products) and the tightening of SMCR controls around AI use, combined with the ICO AI Auditing Framework published in 2024, have moved enterprise AI buyers from 'show me your SOC 2' to 'show me what you did for this deployment.' Several UK banks (HSBC, NatWest, Lloyds) have publicly stated AI vendor risk frameworks that include per-deployment evidence. The pattern itself is already proven — UK building control has used stage-gate inspection since 1985, and the Building Safety Act 2022 extended the pattern to high-rise residential.
