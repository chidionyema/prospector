FloorCase — the fixed-fee pack that stops Universal Credit treating a gig worker as if they earn full-time wages.

A done-for-you evidence pack and challenge letter for gig workers whose Universal Credit is cut by the Minimum Income Floor, sold for a flat fee.

Universal Credit has a rule most gig workers only discover when their payment drops. If the DWP decides you are 'gainfully self-employed', it can assume you earn 35 hours a week at minimum wage — around £1,900 a month — even in a week when the delivery apps paid you £600. That assumed figure is called the Minimum Income Floor, and it quietly removes hundreds of pounds a month from riders, drivers and freelance side-hustlers on volatile income.

The decisions behind it can be challenged. Whether you count as gainfully self-employed, whether you were given the 12-month start-up period the rules allow, and whether it was ended early are all determinations a claimant can contest through a mandatory reconsideration and then a tribunal. Almost nobody does, because the process is paperwork-shaped and the free advisers who understand it — Citizens Advice, law centres — have waiting lists measured in weeks.

FloorCase productises the challenge. The worker uploads their app earnings statements, bank feed and work-search history; the service assembles a standardised evidence bundle — trading records, hours logs, the correct regulation references — plus a ready-to-send mandatory reconsideration letter, for a fixed fee of £99, with a £249 tier that drafts the tribunal appeal if the reconsideration fails. This borrows the way American property-tax appeal firms built profitable businesses mass-producing challenges to an official's presumed valuation: the state names a number, the firm industrialises the rebuttal. The DWP's presumed income is the same shape of target.

A two-person team can run it: one welfare-rights-literate operator reviewing outputs, one person on intake and templates. Each decided case feeds a growing library of what actually persuades DWP decision-makers and First-tier Tribunal judges, which sharpens the templates and is the one asset a new copycat starts without. Success stories are inherently shareable in the Facebook and Reddit groups where couriers already compare payment screenshots, so acquisition can start at near-zero cost.

UK gig workers on Universal Credit — delivery riders, private-hire drivers, freelance side-hustlers aged roughly 18 to 30 — at the moment a Minimum Income Floor decision cuts their monthly payment. They pay £99 to £249 once, against a loss that can exceed £300 every month.

DWP has been tightening self-employment reviews as the managed migration from tax credits to Universal Credit pushes more self-employed people onto UC through 2025–26, so more gig workers are hitting gainful self-employment interviews and MIF decisions for the first time. Gig platform earnings have also stagnated while the assumed floor rises each April with the National Living Wage, widening the gap between what the DWP imputes and what the apps actually pay.
