SoundLease, track licence money owed on old ads.

For freelance composers and sound designers, this tool watches when a brand's advert is still running past the licence you sold, and produces the invoice for the extra period.

Start from the raw pain rather than from how music licensing is sold today. A freelance composer writes 40 seconds of music for a car brand's advert. The contract grants a one-year UK broadcast and online licence for a flat £3,000. Twelve months later, nobody at the brand or the agency remembers the end date. The advert keeps running on ITV, on YouTube, in cinema pre-rolls and on the brand's own social channels. The composer only finds out by accident, usually years late, usually while watching television.

The reason this happens is structural. The person who signed the licence has left the agency. The media plan lives in a different system from the contract. Nobody is paid to remember when a small supplier's rights expire. So the overrun is invisible to everyone except the one person with no way to see it.

SoundLease is a tool the composer runs on their own catalogue. You upload each track you licensed, plus the plain facts of the deal: who bought it, which territories, which media, and the end date. The tool fingerprints the audio, then watches for that fingerprint appearing after the end date. It checks the buyer's own YouTube and Meta channels, the free UK broadcast monitoring data available through the advert clearance record at Clearcast, and the public ad libraries that Meta and Google are required to keep under the EU Digital Services Act, which covers ads served to UK users by the same global platforms. When the fingerprint shows up past the date, you get a dated evidence file: a screenshot, a timestamp, a link, and the matching clause from your own contract.

Then it produces the money document. Under UK law this is straightforward: using a copyright work outside the terms of the licence is infringement under section 16 of the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988, and the usual commercial remedy is a fee for the extra period. The tool drafts that letter for you, priced against the original deal rather than against a guess, and gives the buyer a simple way to pay and extend rather than argue.

The part that holds is the record you build. Every deal you log, every overrun you catch, and every settlement you agree becomes a private history of what UK brands and agencies actually pay to extend a music licence after the fact. Nobody publishes those numbers, because every settlement is quiet and most are never written down. After a few hundred cases the tool can tell a composer what a two-year overrun on a mid-market retail brand actually settled for last quarter. That figure is what turns an awkward email into a confident invoice, and it cannot be bought or scraped, because it only exists inside the settlements the tool handled.

It is also sticky in a plain way. Once your whole back catalogue and every contract date sits in one place, moving to something else means re-entering years of deals, and the watching stops the moment you leave.

UK freelance composers, sound designers and jingle writers who license music directly to brands and advertising agencies, typically earning £30,000 to £120,000 a year from 5 to 40 deals. They pay a monthly fee of around £29 to watch their catalogue, plus a share of anything recovered. Small independent music libraries and one-person production music companies buy the same thing at a higher tier.

Two things changed. Advertising moved from a handful of dated broadcast slots to always-on social channels, where a video simply stays up unless somebody takes it down, so overruns are now the normal outcome rather than an error. And the ad transparency libraries that Meta and Google now run publish, for the first time, a public and searchable record of which adverts a brand is currently serving and when they started. Audio fingerprinting that used to need a broadcaster's budget now runs on ordinary cloud compute for pennies per hour of audio.
