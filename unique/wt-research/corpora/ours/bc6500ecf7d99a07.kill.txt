AcceptDock — a wrist rest with three big buttons for saying yes or no to AI code suggestions.

A USB wrist rest with three low-force macro buttons pre-mapped to accept, reject and skip AI code suggestions in Copilot, Cursor and Windsurf, sold to developers and claimable against their employer's home-office equipment allowance.

Developers who use GitHub Copilot, Cursor or Windsurf now hit the same three keys hundreds of times a day: Tab to accept a suggestion, Esc to reject it, and a modifier combo to skip to the next one. That is a new, repetitive strain pattern that did not exist five years ago, and it lands on the same pinky and thumb tendons that were already sore from normal typing. This idea puts those three actions on their own oversized, low-travel buttons built into a padded wrist rest, so the hand never has to stretch to a corner key hundreds of times a shift.

It sells as a physical product through a direct-to-consumer site, the way a standing-desk or ergonomic-mouse brand does: one core SKU around £69, a slimmer travel version, and a subscription add-on for replacement palm pads that wear out every six to nine months. A small team of two to ten people can run this — design and firmware stay in-house, injection moulding and PCB assembly go to a contract manufacturer, and a 3PL in the UK handles fulfilment.

The UK-specific hook is real money already sitting on the table: under the Health and Safety (Display Screen Equipment) Regulations 1992, as updated by HSE guidance for home workers since 2020, employers have a duty to assess and provide suitable equipment for staff who use display screens for long periods, including at home. Most tech employers already run a home-office or DSE equipment allowance, typically £150 to £300 a year, that goes unclaimed because ordering a proper ergonomic setup is a hassle. AcceptDock ships with a one-page DSE self-assessment form that names the specific repetitive movement it addresses, making it an easy line item to expense rather than something a developer has to justify from their own pocket.

The part a generic ergonomic-keyboard brand cannot easily copy is the data. The companion app is opt-in and free, and all it does is log which of the three buttons gets hit hardest and how far the hand travels to reach them, across thousands of real coding sessions on real AI tools. That keeps feeding back into button travel distance, spring force and hand-size sizing, and it lets the product add a fourth or fifth mapped action the moment a new AI coding tool changes its default hotkeys, months before a general ergonomics brand would even notice the shift happened.
