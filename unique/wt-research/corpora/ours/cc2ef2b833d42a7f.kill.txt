Model change rehearsals for AI product agencies.

A fixed-fee service that re-runs an agency's client AI features against every new model version before the old one is switched off, and hands over a signed report saying what broke.

Small agencies now build client features on top of Anthropic, OpenAI and Google models. Those models get retired on published dates. Anthropic publishes model deprecations and retirement dates in its docs, and OpenAI publishes a deprecations page. When a model retires, the agency's client work silently changes behaviour, and the agency finds out from an angry client.

The agency owner has no test bench for this. Their code tests check their own code. Nobody checks whether the model still answers the way the client signed off on.

Built from the raw pain rather than from how anyone solves it today: the real asset is not software, it is the frozen record of how each client feature behaved on the day the client accepted it. So the service starts by capturing that. For each client feature, it records a few hundred real inputs and the outputs the client approved. That set is the acceptance record.

Then, every time a supplier announces a new version or a retirement date, the service replays the acceptance record against the new model and writes a short report: which outputs moved, by how much, which ones cross the client's own acceptance line, and what the agency should change before the switch. It is delivered as a document the agency can forward to the client, or attach to a change-request invoice.

This is shape (C), selling to technology companies. The buyer is the agency owner or delivery lead. The recurring trigger is a supplier's published retirement or version date. The deliverable is the replay report plus a recommended fix list. The public source proving this is already paid work is the vendors' own deprecation and model-retirement pages, which give hard dates that force the work into someone's week.

The wedge is the acceptance record itself. Every quarter the service holds more of it, across more clients, and after two cycles it can tell an agency which kinds of prompts historically break on a version bump before running anything. Rivals starting later have no history. It also creates a nasty rip-out cost: the acceptance evidence for every client feature lives here.

Monetisation is per client feature per quarter, roughly £400 to £900 a feature, with an urgent single-report price when a retirement date lands inside four weeks. An agency with fifteen live AI features pays real money and bills most of it on to clients as maintenance.

A solo operator runs it because capture and replay are scripted, and only the written judgement at the end needs a person.

Owners and delivery leads of UK digital agencies with roughly five to forty staff who have shipped AI features for clients and now carry support retainers on them. They pay today by absorbing the emergency rebuild hours themselves.

Model suppliers have started publishing firm retirement dates for older versions, so agencies face a scheduled break rather than a vague risk. At the same time UK agencies have shipped a first wave of client AI features that are now old enough to be sitting on models with an end date.
