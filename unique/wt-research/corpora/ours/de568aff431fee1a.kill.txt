SwapSeat — the auction where families sell an unwanted school transport place back to the council's waiting list.

A booking exchange where a carer who no longer needs their child's council-funded taxi seat hands it to a waiting family, and the transport operator pays a fee to keep the vehicle full.

Here is the plain version. Councils in England must arrange free home-to-school transport for eligible children, mostly under sections 508B and 509AA of the Education Act 1996 and the statutory home-to-school travel guidance. For children with special needs this usually means a taxi or minibus seat, contracted out to a local operator on a per-seat, per-day route price. Once a route is set, the seat is paid for whether the child rides or not.

Carers know the seat is theirs and that giving it up is a one-way door. So they hold empty seats through hospital stays, school refusal, part-time timetables and shared custody weeks. Meanwhile another family sits on the waiting list, running their own child in by car and losing paid hours doing it.

SwapSeat is a place where those two families meet, with the operator in the middle. A carer lists the days their seat is genuinely free. A waiting family books it for a term or a half term. The operator confirms the route works and takes the booking. The carer gets a written guarantee that their underlying entitlement is untouched, plus a small payment per released day. The operator pays SwapSeat a fee on every filled seat, because a full vehicle is the difference between a profitable route and a loss-making one.

This is the inverting move. Councils are under enormous pressure on this budget and are getting more aggressive about eligibility, personal budgets and route reviews. A council can publish rules and run its own waiting list. It cannot take a commercial side, cannot pay one parent to release a seat another parent is entitled to, and cannot act for the family against its own transport team. The more a council squeezes, the more valuable a place that quietly rematches seats becomes.

What holds is the route data. Every filled seat teaches SwapSeat which postcodes, which schools, which pickup windows and which wheelchair configurations actually fit together on a real vehicle. That matching record cannot be bought and grows with every term. Operators come back because the tool tells them, before the tender, which seats on which routes will sit empty.

Growth runs through the operators themselves. Fewer than 300 firms hold most of the special needs school transport contracts in England, and each one carries hundreds of families. Sign the operator, and the whole route list gets invited.

The special needs school transport operators holding council contracts pay a fee for every seat they fill through the exchange, typically firms running 20 to 200 vehicles for one or two county councils. Carers pay nothing and receive a small payment for each day they release.

Councils in England spent over £1.8bn on home-to-school transport for children with special educational needs in 2023-24, and the figure has roughly doubled in five years. Several county councils have declared this the single fastest-growing line in their budget. That has triggered mass route reviews, personal travel budget pushes and tighter eligibility checks. Operators are being squeezed on route price at the same moment, so an empty seat now hurts them directly.
