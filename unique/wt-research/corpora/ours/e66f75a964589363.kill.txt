TrainWatch, finds your art inside AI models and proves it.

TrainWatch fingerprints a freelance creative's work at export, then hunts AI models and datasets for matches to build a UK copyright claim.

A UK freelance illustrator, designer or copywriter publishes work constantly — a Figma export, a portfolio upload, a client deliverable — with no record that proves it was theirs first, and no way of knowing if it later turns up inside an AI model's training set or its output. Right now the only options are prospective and voluntary: cloaking tools like Glaze or Nightshade scramble an image before upload so a scraper reads it wrong, and opt-out lists like Spawning.ai's 'Have I Been Trained' ask dataset compilers to honour a do-not-train flag. Neither tells you what already happened to the ten years of work you published before you'd heard of either tool, and neither builds you a case.

TrainWatch works the other way round. A plugin for Figma, Adobe Creative Cloud, Word and Google Docs stamps every export with a timestamped perceptual hash and a personal, private fingerprint vault — proof of exactly what you made and when, sitting on your own machine, not a public registry. A background service then periodically tests your fingerprints against outputs from major image and text models and against public indexes of known training sets (LAION-5B derivatives, Common Crawl news/web dumps). When it finds a match above a confidence threshold, it assembles a bundle a solicitor or a platform's rights-takedown desk can actually use: the original file's creation timestamp, the hash comparison, the model or dataset the match came from, and the relevant sections of the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 — including the section 29A text-and-data-mining exception, which only protects non-commercial research and so does not cover most commercial model training.

The moat is the vault itself. Every freelancer who fingerprints their work from day one adds confirmed match cases — instances where a specific hash was found in a specific model's output — to a growing ground-truth set that makes the next match faster and more confident to verify. A rival starting today has no history of matches to train against and no base of freelancers who fingerprinted their work years before the rival existed; the value compounds with time in the vault, not with feature parity.

Freelance illustrators, designers, photographers and copywriters in the UK who sell usage rights for a living and feel the ground shift under them every time a client mentions Midjourney. They already pay for Adobe Creative Cloud and stock licensing tools, so a monitoring subscription sits in familiar budget territory, and the fear is concrete enough — a client asking 'can't AI just do this' — that they'll pay to have an answer ready.

Getty Images' UK High Court case against Stability AI, heard through 2024 and 2025, put a specific UK legal question in front of working creatives for the first time: does training a model on copyrighted images made and hosted in the UK infringe copyright here, even if the training happened partly abroad. The UK IPO's 2022–2023 consultation on an AI text-and-data-mining exception stalled without a clear new law, leaving section 29A's non-commercial-research limit as the live rule freelancers can actually cite. At the same time, Adobe's push for C2PA content-credential provenance standards has made 'timestamped proof of authorship' something creatives are newly used to attaching to their work, which is exactly the habit TrainWatch needs them to already have.

The one UK court case that tested this exact legal question — using training-data matches to found a copyright claim against an AI developer — ended with the High Court rejecting Getty Images' secondary copyright infringement claim against Stability AI, meaning even a well-resourced rights holder with a strong dataset could not win the kind of claim TrainWatch's evidence bundles are meant to feed.
