Screen-reader test crews for UK app teams.

A booked panel of blind and low-vision testers who run your product with their own screen readers and hand your engineers a fix list mapped to the exact WCAG rule your public-sector buyer checks.

This sells picks and shovels to software companies: the tested-by-real-users evidence they need, not another scanner. A product manager inside a UK software firm bidding for public-sector work must publish an accessibility statement and meet the Public Sector Bodies (Websites and Mobile Applications) (No. 2) Accessibility Regulations 2018, which point at WCAG 2.2 AA. Automated scanners catch roughly a third of the failures. The rest only show up when a person using JAWS, NVDA or VoiceOver actually tries to finish a task.

The product is a scheduled session: three testers with different assistive setups, a recorded run through the flows you name, and a defect list written as a ticket an engineer can close, each line tied to the exact success criterion. Turnaround is five working days. Price is a fixed fee per release, sold as a seat on a monthly slot so it repeats every sprint.

This borrows the way clinical trials pay a standing panel of participants rather than recruiting fresh each time. Nobody applies that to software testing, where recruitment is treated as a one-off scramble.

The compounding asset is the panel plus the defect history. After a few hundred sessions you know which UI patterns break which screen reader at which version, and you can warn a client before they ship. A rival starting today has no panel and no history. Growth is product-led at the bottom (self-serve booking of a single session from the website) and forced at the top: every UK public-sector tender and every Digital Marketplace listing asks for the evidence, so buyers arrive already knowing they need it.

Product managers and engineering leads inside UK software companies with 1-20 staff who sell to councils, NHS trusts and central government, and who must produce an accessibility statement before their bid is accepted. They typically hold a release budget of a few thousand pounds a quarter and currently pay a consultancy four-figure sums for a one-off audit.

The Public Sector Bodies Accessibility Regulations 2018 require public bodies to publish an accessibility statement, and the Government Digital Service monitors and reports on compliance. WCAG 2.2 became the W3C recommendation in October 2023 and UK guidance on gov.uk now points at it, so every statement written against 2.1 is out of date and needs re-testing. Meanwhile the European Accessibility Act applies from June 2025, which pulls UK firms selling into the EU into the same testing need.
