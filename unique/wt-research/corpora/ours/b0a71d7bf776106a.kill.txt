Second Sitting — sells your unused school fees in credit to another family, at a price the school has to honour.

When a private school owes your family fee credit you will never use, we find another parent who wants it and broker the swap for cash.

Start with the plain version. Independent schools in the UK routinely end up holding money that belongs to a family who has left or is leaving. It comes from fees paid a term in advance, from the Fees in Advance schemes many schools ran before January 2025, from sibling discounts applied late, and from deposits held against a place that was never taken up. Schools return this money slowly, in their own time, and often only as credit against a bill that no longer exists.

This business sits between two families and gets paid to move that money. A parent leaving the school has £4,000 of credit and no child to spend it on. A parent staying has a £4,000 bill due in September. We buy the leaver's credit at a discount, sell it to the stayer at a smaller discount, and take the spread. Neither family deals with the other. We take the risk that the school honours the transfer, and we price that risk.

Why this can be built rather than merely imagined. The moment a school agrees once to move credit between two accounts on our instruction, it has told us something no rival knows: this school will do it, on these terms, within this many days, signed off by this bursar. That record grows with every deal. After a hundred trades across forty schools we can quote a leaving parent a firm cash price in an hour, because we know which schools settle and which stall. A newcomer with no trade history has to guess, and guessing wrong on the first ten deals costs real money.

The angle here is deliberately contrary. Most people building around private school fees are trying to help families comply with the VAT change or find a cheaper school. We want the opposite. The more aggressively schools tighten their terms, the harder it becomes for a family to get its own money back, and the more a firm cash offer today is worth. Our value grows with the school's inflexibility. A school cannot build this itself, because a school can only ever act for its own accounts and has every reason to keep the credit sitting where it is.

On the legal side, this is an assignment of a debt between consenting parties with the school's written agreement, which is ordinary commercial ground. We do not hold client money longer than a settlement window, we do not lend, and we do not advise on tax. We would take advice on whether the discount element engages any consumer credit rules and structure the purchase as an outright assignment rather than an advance.

Growth comes from the leaving side, which is easy to find. Parents announce departures on school forums, in local Facebook groups, and to the very education consultants who handle moves. Those consultants become our first paid channel: they already know, months ahead, exactly which families are leaving and which are arriving.

Two named payers. First, parents aged 35-55 leaving a UK independent school mid-cycle who are owed fee credit or an advance-fee balance and want cash now rather than a refund in eight months. Second, parents already at that same school facing a termly bill of £6,000 or more who will happily pay £5,600 in cash for £6,000 of credit. We are paid from the spread on every completed transfer, typically £300 to £900 per trade.

From 1 January 2025 the UK government applied 20% VAT to private school fees, ending the exemption. Schools that had collected fees years in advance under Fees in Advance schemes were left holding large balances with disputed VAT treatment, and HMRC's anti-forestalling rules from 29 July 2024 caught many of those payments. Fee increases since have pushed families out mid-cycle in unusual numbers, so more accounts than ever are sitting in credit with nobody attached to them. At the same time, bursars are under real cash pressure and are slower than ever to refund.
