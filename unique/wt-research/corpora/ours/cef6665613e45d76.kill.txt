PermitPin — the map that tells a street food trader where they can actually trade next week.

A self-serve tool that shows mobile food traders every UK council pitch, market and event they can legally trade at, with the licence they need and the deadline to apply.

A person running a coffee van or a burger trailer spends more time hunting for places to trade than cooking. Every one of the 333 councils in England, plus the 32 Scottish and 22 Welsh ones, runs its own street trading consent system under the Local Government (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1982 and, in London, the London Local Authorities Act 1990. Each publishes its own rules, its own fees and its own designated streets list, in its own format, usually as a PDF buried three clicks deep. There is no single place to see them.

PermitPin is a map. You type your postcode and how far you will travel. It shows every pitch within that radius: the council, the exact street or site, the consent type needed, the fee, whether applications are open, and the date they close. Free users see the pitches. Paying users get the deadline alerts, the saved application answers that auto-fill each council's form, and the record of which pitches actually had traders on them last season.

This borrows the way rig-permit and drilling-rights data firms in oil and gas turned scattered county filings into a subscription that operators cannot work without. Nobody has applied that to street trading, because the parcels are small and the payers were assumed to be poor. They are not: a decent pitch is worth £600 to £2,000 a week in takings, so £29 a month to find three more is an easy sum.

The part that compounds is the outcome data. Every user who applies tells the tool whether they got the consent, how long it took, and what the council actually asked for. After two seasons you know which councils grant in ten days and which take four months, which pitches turn over, and which events reject newcomers. No council has that view, because no council sees the other 386. That record cannot be scraped or bought — it only exists because the traders using the tool report back.

Growth is product-led and geographic. Traders cluster: they queue at the same cash-and-carries, share the same Facebook groups, and park at the same overnight spots. One trader in a town finds a pitch, tells four others, and the free map spreads. Paid conversion happens at the moment a deadline is about to close.

It holds because switching away means going back to phoning councils one at a time. Once a trader's licence documents, insurance certificates, food hygiene rating and standard application answers live in the tool, every new application is one click instead of an afternoon.

Self-employed mobile caterers and market traders — coffee vans, burger trailers, dessert carts, hot food units — typically one to three people, turning over £40,000 to £180,000 a year. They pay £29 a month, or £249 a year, out of the marketing budget they currently spend on nothing. Event organisers and market operators pay a separate listing fee to put open pitches in front of the traders.

Street trading has moved outdoors and mobile since 2020, and councils have leaned into it: many now run food markets and meanwhile-use schemes on car parks and high streets they could not fill with shops. At the same time, the Business and Planning Act 2020 pavement licence route was made permanent in England by the Levelling-up and Regeneration Act 2023, so councils publish more site data than they used to. The number of separate places a trader could apply to has grown, and so has the time cost of finding them.
