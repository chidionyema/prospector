SectorHedge, cash when your sector's funding dries up.

A parametric policy that pays a UK startup cash the moment deal volume in its specific sector collapses, so a founder can extend runway without an emergency down round.

A founder running a UK seed or Series A company doesn't fail because their product is bad. They fail because the specific pocket of VC money that funds their sector dries up for six months right when they need to raise, and there's nothing they can buy today that pays out on that.

SectorHedge borrows the mechanism crop insurers use in agriculture: instead of assessing an individual farm's loss after a drought, they pay out automatically once a public rainfall index falls below a set level, no loss adjuster, no argument. SectorHedge applies the same idea to venture funding. It builds a live index of UK deal volume and deal value by narrow sector, using Companies House SH01 (allotment of shares) filings, HMRC's SEIS/EIS advance assurance data, and Beauhurst's and the British Business Bank's published quarterly equity investment breakdowns, refreshed as fast as those sources update. A founder buys a policy at seed stage tied to their own sector code (say, 'UK climate hardware' or 'UK vertical SaaS for healthcare'). If the index shows deal count in that sector drops past an agreed threshold for a defined window, ahead of when the founder is due to raise, the policy pays out a fixed cash sum automatically, no claims process, because the trigger is the public index itself, not a company-specific loss.

The business isn't the startup itself becoming an insurer. It's structured as a managing general agent (MGA), the same route Zego and Marshmallow used to get to market before ever holding their own balance sheet: SectorHedge builds the underwriting model and binds capacity from a Lloyd's syndicate or specialty reinsurer who takes the tail risk, while SectorHedge keeps the pricing algorithm, the distribution relationship with founders and VCs, and the growing correlation dataset linking sector funding-drought severity to actual startup down-round and shutdown rates. That correlation dataset is the compounding asset: every policy written and every renewal adds a data point on how a funding drought in one narrow UK sector actually translated into founder outcomes, which is not something Beauhurst or PitchBook, who sell data subscriptions, have any reason or licence to build, and not something a general commercial insurer's named-peril underwriting process is set up to price.

Distribution runs through the channel VCs already use to protect their own portfolios: a fund buys a block policy covering its portfolio companies in a given sector as part of its downside-protection pitch to LPs, which gives SectorHedge a repeatable, partner-led growth motion rather than needing to sell founder by founder.

UK seed and Series A founders who are 6-18 months from their next raise pay the premium directly, typically £2,000 to £8,000 a year depending on sector volatility and cover level. In practice, many policies get bought in bulk by the VC funds backing them, who add it to their portfolio-support package because a funding-drought payout reduces the number of portfolio companies forced into a fire-sale down round.

Beauhurst and the British Business Bank both publish granular, sector-coded UK equity investment data every quarter, which only became detailed and timely enough in the last few years to build a live trigger index from, rather than a lagging annual report. At the same time, the 2023 SEIS/EIS limit increases pushed far more early-stage UK deal activity through advance-assurance filings that HMRC processes and that can be tracked as a leading indicator, giving SectorHedge a second, faster data feed than deal announcements alone. Lloyd's of London has also been actively expanding delegated binding authority to smaller MGAs building novel parametric products outside the traditional weather and crop space, which is the specific capacity relationship this business needs to exist at all.
