Bin store recycling signs for small flat landlords.

Printed, weatherproof bin store signs made for one specific block of flats, showing exactly which bin each item goes in under that council's own rules.

This is a physical product sold online, not a service. A landlord or managing agent types in the block's postcode and the number of bins in the store. They get back a set of rigid, weatherproof boards, sized to the bin store wall, showing that council's exact collection rules in pictures.

Start from the raw pain rather than how it is handled today. Councils in England can serve a written notice on the person responsible for a property, setting out how household waste must be presented (this comes from section 46 of the Environmental Protection Act 1990). Breaching it can lead to a fixed penalty. For a small landlord with a converted house or a six-flat block, the trigger is usually contamination: one wrong bag, the whole bin is rejected, rubbish piles up, tenants complain, and the council escalates.

The landlord's real problem is not knowing the rules. It is that no two councils sort waste the same way, and generic 'recycling' stickers from a builders' merchant are useless. Birmingham, Manchester and Camden each accept different plastics, use different bin colours, and collect on different cycles. A generic sign tells a tenant nothing about what happens in their bin store.

The compounding asset is the drawing library. Every council in England publishes its accepted-materials list and bin colours on its own website, and there are over 300 collection authorities. Turning each into a clean, tested, picture-first board is slow, dull, repetitive work. Once done, that library is owned artwork, reusable forever, and it gets better each time a customer sends back a photo of what their tenants actually got wrong.

A solo operator runs this with a print-on-demand rigid board supplier who ships direct to the customer. No stock, no unit, no staff. The design file is generated from the council record; the printer does the rest.

Pricing sits at £45 for a two-board set, £85 for a full bin store kit with bin-lid stickers and a laminated tenant handout. Repeat purchase comes from tenant turnover, vandalism, and councils changing their rules — which they do, often, as new recycling duties phase in.

Distribution is owned and free. Landlord forums, local landlord association Facebook groups, and the property management subreddits are full of people posting photos of rejected bins. A searchable page per council ('Camden bin store signs', 'Leeds bin store signs') captures people already looking for the answer.

Small residential landlords and managing agents in England who look after between two and thirty flats, especially those in converted houses and small blocks where one shared bin store serves every tenant. They buy after a rejected collection, a neighbour complaint, or a council warning letter.

England's recycling rules are being made uniform for workplaces and are changing for households under the Environment Act 2021, so councils are actively rewriting what goes in which bin and telling residents about it. At the same time, councils under budget pressure are using fixed penalties for waste offences more readily. Landlords are caught between rules they did not write and tenants who cannot read a generic sticker.
