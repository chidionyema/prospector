Fee Harbour, places your child before the school folds.

A discreet broker that moves your child into a stable school within days when your private school shows signs of closing, paid only when placed.

Since VAT was added to private school fees in January 2025, small and mid-sized independent schools across the UK have been closing mid-year, often with only a few weeks' notice to parents. Families who paid a term's fees upfront lose that money and scramble to find a new school while term is already underway, competing with dozens of other displaced families for the same handful of spare places.

This borrows the way an airline handling IRROPS rebooks passengers the moment a flight is cancelled, rather than waiting for them to queue at the desk, and applies it to school places: the broker watches for the warning signs before the closure notice goes out, and already has a placement lined up the day it does.

The business tracks financial distress signals that compound over time and are hard to assemble from scratch: Companies House filings and charges registered against schools run as companies, Charity Commission accounts and reserves levels for the many schools that are charities, Independent Schools Inspectorate reports, ISC census entries on falling rolls, and patterns from the wave of closures already seen in 2024 and 2025. Cross-referenced against a private network of bursars and heads at schools with spare capacity, this produces an early list of schools at risk and the specific nearby schools that could take their pupils.

When a school does announce closure, or a family senses one is coming, the broker makes the introduction to a school with a matching place, handles the fee-waiver conversation, and takes a fixed placement fee only once the child is enrolled. Receiving schools pay a smaller finder's fee too, because filling an empty desk fast without running a public marketing campaign is worth something to them.

Neither the Department for Education, the ISI, nor the Charity Commission can build this: naming a specific school as financially distressed before it fails can itself trigger the run that causes the failure, so regulators structurally have to stay silent until the closure is already public. That silence is exactly the gap this fills.

Parents already paying five-figure annual private school fees, who pay a fixed placement fee (roughly £750–£1,500) only once their child has an offer at a new school. Receiving schools with spare capacity also pay a smaller fee for a fast, no-marketing-spend admission.
