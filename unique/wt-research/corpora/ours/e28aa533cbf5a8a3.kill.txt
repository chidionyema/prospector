SwapShift — the platform where NHS and care staff sell shifts they can't work to colleagues who want them.

A booking platform for family carers working in health and social care: list a shift you can no longer cover, and a verified colleague at the same employer takes it, with pay transferred automatically.

Start from the raw pain, not from how rostering is done today. A person caring for a parent with dementia works Tuesday nights on a hospital bank or a care home rota. The parent has a bad week. That carer must either drop the shift and lose the money, or leave the person they care for alone. Today the only route is a WhatsApp group and a manager's goodwill, and the money is lost either way.

SwapShift is a marketplace for the shift itself. You list Tuesday night. A colleague on the same employer's staff bank, already cleared for that ward or home, claims it. The employer's roster is updated through the same booking, and SwapShift settles the money: the person who gives up the shift keeps a small fee, the person who works it is paid for it. The platform takes a percentage of each swap.

The reason this is a transaction and not a message board is that both sides need certainty at the moment of the swap. The carer needs to know the shift is truly covered before they can commit to their relative. The taker needs to know they will be paid and will not be turned away at the door. Only a platform holding both sides of the booking can promise that.

The part that gets harder to copy over time is the matching data. Every completed swap teaches SwapShift which grades, wards, homes and travel distances actually convert, and which listings die unclaimed. After a few thousand swaps in one trust or care group, it can tell a carer at listing time whether a Tuesday night in that unit will clear, and at what fee. A newcomer starts with no history of who took what.

Distribution is the strongest part. Swapping is inherently two-sided inside one workplace: every listing invites colleagues in, and every taker becomes a lister. Adoption spreads ward by ward without a sales call, then the employer signs a paid agreement to make the roster update official. In England the Care Quality Commission expects providers to show that staff on duty are suitably qualified and that records match reality, so an employer has a real reason to prefer a swap that leaves an audit trail over a WhatsApp handover.

Two payers. Health and care workers who are also unpaid family carers pay a percentage of each shift they hand over — money they would otherwise lose entirely. Employers pay a per-site subscription: NHS trust temporary staffing teams running their own staff bank, and independent care home groups of roughly 5 to 40 homes, who currently pay agency rates when a shift falls through.

Carers UK and NHS England both report large numbers of staff leaving because caring at home cannot bend around a fixed rota. NHS trusts have been pushed to cut agency spend and move work onto their own staff banks, so a shift that falls through now costs the trust an expensive agency booking. The Carer's Leave Act 2023 gave employees a right to unpaid carer's leave from April 2024, which makes short-notice caring absence a normal, discussable event rather than a hidden one — but unpaid leave does not pay a mortgage, and swapping the shift does.

Carers UK research shows 2.6 million people in the UK have quit their jobs to care for an older, disabled or seriously ill relative, with over 600 leaving work each day, and estimates that about 32% of NHS staff — roughly half a million people — are themselves providing unpaid care, rising to 43% of those aged 51-65 [d61dbddb05f5a152][0b805319461c79b4]. That is direct evidence that health service workers face an acute, costly clash between fixed rotas and caring at home, severe enough that large numbers give up paid income over it — the exact pain this candidate targets, though the passages…

The passages show NHS trusts under savings pressure and shifting how they buy temporary staffing, and show care home operators running on very thin profits (under 3% in some homes, under 1% in one analysis) with high debt and a large share of beds financially fragile — which raises doubt about care groups funding a new subscription, but none of the passages say what trusts or care homes actually spend on rostering, staff banks or agency cover, nor whether they buy tools like this.

The passages show that NHS staff already book shifts through digital platforms they sign up to themselves — Patchwork is used by a Trust's staff bank, Locum's Nest lets healthcare professionals pick up work directly, and one service lets staff book bank shifts across multiple trusts without paperwork friction. That establishes an existing, worker-facing booking channel of exactly the kind SwapShift would need to reach the same people, though no passage speaks to how easily a new entrant could get in front of those workers.

The passages state that UK law bars a business that finds people work from charging those workers a fee for it, with an exception only for entertainment and modelling [abfff699d12eb4c6, e5dda59601955567, 0778c5fd21deb1d6], and the candidate's stated income is a percentage taken from the health and care workers on each shift it matches. Taking a cut of the worker's own pay for placing them into a shift is the conduct these rules prohibit, so that part of the income cannot be earned as described, even though the separate fee charged to employers is untouched by these passages.
