CellScan — on-the-spot battery health check and shift-endurance forecast for e-bike delivery riders.

A mobile service that tests a delivery rider's e-bike battery on the spot and gives a plain-English report on how many deliveries it can still handle that day, for a fixed fee.

The idea is simple. A delivery rider on an e-bike depends on their battery lasting the whole shift. If it dies early, they lose money and may not know when to replace it. A solo operator with a portable battery load tester and a phone app visits the rider at their usual waiting spot — outside a McDonald's in a busy city centre. They connect a small device to the battery, run a short test, and immediately give the rider a one-page report showing the current health of the battery in terms they understand: 'estimated full-throttle range remaining today with your current load', and a clear recommendation to keep going, swap batteries at lunch, or replace soon. The operator charges £15 per test, cash or card. The service takes ten minutes.

The value is not just the test; it is the data. Each test records the battery's make, model, age, charge cycles, and measured internal resistance and capacity. Over hundreds of tests, the operator builds a proprietary dataset of real-world degradation curves for the batteries that delivery riders actually use — Bosch, Shimano, unbranded Chinese packs. This data is what a standalone battery manufacturer, local bike shop, or even the rider's platform (which may want to offer battery replacement schemes) will pay for. It is also what lets the operator give increasingly accurate shift-endurance forecasts. The model is borrowed from the vehicle breakdown services that test car batteries on the roadside; nobody has applied it to the e-bike delivery workforce systematically.

The wedge is proprietary data. No single competitor can quickly replicate two years' worth of per-ride battery prints across the top five e-bike battery types in actual delivery use. The operator's data becomes the authoritative map of when a battery really needs swapping, which nobody else — not even the manufacturer — has for this very specific use case. The hardest part of the business is getting the first hundred tests. That can be done by spending mornings at the food-court hotspots where riders queue, offering the first test free in exchange for an honest review in the rider WhatsApp groups. After that, word of mouth and the visible value — a folded card that says 'your battery has 78% of its original capacity, you can expect 6.2 hours of light riding' — will bring repeat and new customers. A single operator using a £200 battery analyzer and a smartphone can do 8–10 tests a day, yielding £120–150 gross, with near-zero consumables cost.

Delivery riders who rely on e-bikes, aged 18–27, working for Uber Eats, Deliveroo, Just Eat in UK cities. They pay from their daily earnings, so £15 for immediate peace of mind is a small fraction of a shift's pay. They buy because a test lets them plan their day, avoid being stranded, and avoid buying a new battery too early or too late.

The UK's e-bike delivery fleet has exploded since 2020. The batteries in those bikes are now 2–3 years old and degrading in ways the rider can't see. Meanwhile, the cost of a replacement battery (£300–£600) is a serious hit. No existing service targets these riders with a cheap, instant, human-readable battery health check. The large motoring associations do not cover bicycles, and bike shops want to sell a new battery, not give an impartial measurement. There is a gap.
