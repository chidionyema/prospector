RiderBack — the 24-hour cover that gets UK delivery riders back to work when bike, phone, kit or platform fails.

A weekly subscription that delivers a working bike, a hire phone, a legal defence and a daily stipend to UK delivery riders within 24 hours of any income-stopping failure.

UK delivery riders earn roughly £6 to £10 per active hour on Deliveroo, Uber Eats, Just Eat, Stuart and Amazon Flex. Their income depends on four things they do not own: a working bike or scooter, a working smartphone, the right kit (thermal bag, mount, hi-vis) and an active platform account. If any one of those fails, they earn £0 that day.

The most common failures are predictable and seasonal. AA and TfL data show bike punctures spike in the wetter half of the year; phone theft clusters around weekend nights in city centres; thermal bags fail after six to nine months of daily use; and platform deactivations hit thousands of riders a year, often triggered by opaque algorithmic flags. When any of these happens, the rider typically loses one to three days of income while they wait for a repair, hunt a replacement or work out the platform's dispute form.

RiderBack bundles recovery for all four failure modes into a single weekly subscription of £4.50. Within 24 hours of any failure, the rider gets a hire bike delivered or a mobile mechanic on-site (up to £200 a year of labour and parts), a hire phone via next-day courier if theirs is stolen or cracked, a daily £30 stipend for up to seven days while we pursue the platform deactivation through its own dispute process and, where relevant, a UK GDPR Article 22 challenge to the automated decision, plus replacement thermal, mount or hi-vis up to £300 a year if stolen or damaged on shift.

The model borrows directly from the breakdown cover industry — AA, RAC, Green Flag — where a low annual fee covers a curated network of independent suppliers paid per referral rather than employed. Bike mechanics are local shops on commission; phone hire comes from a partner refurbisher; the deactivation defence comes from a panel firm on a no-win-no-fee. The productised wrap is what an incumbent insurer structurally cannot replicate. AA and RAC are built around motor vehicles, not pedal bikes or e-scooters. Income protection insurance pays out after a four-week waiting period and only for serious illness or injury, not a stolen thermal bag. Mobile phone insurance only covers the phone, not the platform account, the kit or the bike. The platforms' own dispute forms are free, but they are forms: no one chases the deadline, no one writes the appeal, no one argues the automated-decision point under UK GDPR.

Unit economics scale cleanly. Two thousand paying subscribers at £4.50 a week is £9,000 a week, or £468,000 a year of revenue. Supplier costs (mechanic commission, phone refurbisher fee, legal referral fee, stipend payouts) run at roughly 40 to 55 per cent of revenue, leaving £200,000 to £280,000 of gross margin for a team of four to six: an operations manager, two customer support staff, a partnerships lead and a part-time marketer. Acquisition is cheap because the audience is concentrated: thousands of riders in every UK city, findable on Facebook groups, subreddit r/deliveroo, TikTok creator pages and at the rider zones outside muggier restaurants.

The recurring nature of the subscription, plus the muscle memory of having used the service once, is what keeps the churn rate down. The supplementary proprietary asset is the anonymised failure data we collect — which bike models fail most, which postcodes see most deactivations, which thermal bags last — which we can license to insurers, to the platforms themselves, or to kit makers once we have scale.

UK delivery riders working full-time or part-time on Deliveroo, Uber Eats, Just Eat, Stuart and Amazon Flex in cities with 50 or more active couriers, typically aged 18 to 35, living in rented rooms in Birmingham, Manchester, Leeds, Bristol, London and Glasgow.

Active UK courier numbers have grown past 100,000 since 2021 (ONS Labour Force Survey indicators on the gig economy). Platform reliability has worsened in 2023 and 2024 as operators moved to algorithmic deactivation with limited human review, and several high-profile mass-deactivation incidents have been covered in the Guardian and by the GMB union. At the same time, the cost-of-living squeeze means a single missed shift now risks cascading problems — missed rent, overdraft fees, defaulted subscriptions — so a £30 a day buffer has gone from a luxury to a necessity. Thefts of e-bikes in UK cities rose sharply through 2022 and 2023, and Police-recorded crime data shows recovery rates below 10 per cent, leaving riders to absorb the full cost alone.
