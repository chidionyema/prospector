HireLens, AI hiring dossiers from public bias audits.

For an ops manager at a US software firm picking an AI hiring tool, the platform returns normalised vendor dossiers from public bias audits, flagged state by state.

This applies the way contractor-licence aggregators pull state licensing data out of thirty different boards into one normalised lookup, and applies it to AI hiring tools under US state bias-audit laws.

When an ops manager at a mid-sized US software company (50-500 employees, based in New York, Colorado, California, Illinois, Texas, or another active state) needs to choose or renew an AI hiring tool such as HireVue, Modern Hire, Pymetrics, or Eightfold, they spend three to six weeks reading PDFs. Each US state with active AI hiring regulation publishes its own bias audit filings: New York City Local Law 144 (in force since 5 July 2023), the Colorado AI Act (Senate Bill 24-205, staged effective dates running to 1 February 2026 for high-risk systems including employment AI), the Illinois AI Video Interview Act (in force since 2020), plus EEOC settlement press releases, FTC AI-washing actions, and state attorneys general releases on AI hiring vendors. The data is public but scattered across hundreds of PDFs in nine jurisdictions, with no consistent format, no model-version tracking across filings, and no way to compare one vendor's published record against another's.

The platform ingests the filings nightly and normalises the structure: vendor, tool name, model version, audit window, population demographics, impact ratio for protected groups (selection rate, four-fifths rule, adverse-impact ratio), audit firm, filing date, and jurisdiction. It tracks each vendor's published record over time. When the ops manager types a vendor name into the platform, the platform returns the full cross-jurisdiction dossier with year-over-year red flags: selection-rate drops, unexplained impact-ratio swings, audit-firm changes, model swaps between filings, and any enforcement actions tied to that vendor.

Subscriptions are paid per annual cycle, between $9,500 and $24,000 depending on access tier, with renewal alerts keyed to the next state filing deadline (Colorado high-risk provisions, the NYC LL144 annual cycle, EEOC settlement reporting cycles).

The asset compounds with enforcement intensity. Every new state that mandates a bias audit filing adds rows. Every EEOC or state AG action against a named AI hiring vendor adds ground truth. State regulators structurally cannot ship the same product: the NYC Department of Consumer and Worker Protection publishes only raw PDFs; the Colorado Civil Rights Division cannot take a commercial side; no regulator can name the safer vendor, arbitrate between two states' filing formats, or track a vendor across a model swap or an acquisition.

The incumbents most often confused with this — Credo AI, Holistic AI, Monitaur — sell internal tooling for the regulated firm to run its own bias audit on its own workforce. They sit on the buyer side and do not aggregate other firms' published audits. That gap is structural: an internal audit-tool vendor cannot repurpose into a third-party benchmark without breaking the independence rules that audit firms (such as Bender Consulting or the academic AI bias test groups) have to maintain.

The growth motion is partner-led, into AI procurement playbooks at mid-market employment-law firms (Fisher Phillips, Jackson Lewis, Littler Mendelson) and into HR-tech diligence arms (Mercer, Aon). Once the dossier is cited in a client memo, it becomes the working reference, and the next renewal trigger is the next state filing deadline.

They have a $10,000-$30,000 annual budget line for AI compliance research, and they re-evaluate the tool each year because bias audits re-file annually or the law expands.

And the SEC's 2024 AI-wash sweep has reset the vendor-disclosure posture at every software company that sells into enterprises, multiplying the filings on the consumer side as well.
