Overload defence for self-employed tipper and grab drivers.

A phone tool for self-employed tipper and grab lorry drivers that records every load, so an overload fine gets challenged and the loading site pays for it instead.

A self-employed tipper driver arrives at a quarry or a demolition site. Someone else operates the loading shovel. The driver never sees the weight until a DVSA weighbridge check on the A14 says the axle is over. The fine, the prohibition notice, and the lost day are all the driver's, even though the driver did not put the muck in the body.

TipperWatch is a phone tool the driver runs on every job. It captures three things at the point of loading: a timestamped photo of the load and the loading plant, the site's own weighbridge ticket or a note that no ticket was offered, and a GPS-stamped record of who signed the job off. It builds a running weight history per vehicle, per site, and per loader operator.

When a fine lands, the tool assembles two documents. One is a written representation to the DVSA or the court arguing that the driver took all reasonable steps, which is the actual defence available under section 41B of the Road Traffic Act 1988 for overloading. The other is a demand letter to the loading site, because the operator who loaded and dispatched an overweight vehicle is separately liable for using and permitting under the same Act, and DVSA's own enforcement policy names consignors and loaders as targets.

Here is the turn that makes it work. Everyone assumes enforcement is the enemy of the driver. It is not. DVSA has been steadily widening who it prosecutes up the supply chain, and every year of tougher enforcement makes the driver's evidence of who actually loaded the lorry worth more, not less. The tool gets more valuable the harder DVSA pushes, because it is the only record that moves the blame to the party with the money. DVSA structurally cannot build this. It is the prosecutor. It cannot hold a driver's private evidence, take the driver's side, or help one party in a case it brings.

The compounding asset is the load data. After a few thousand jobs, TipperWatch knows which quarries and demolition sites habitually dispatch heavy, which loader operators are the worst, and what the real weight distribution looks like per body type and material. That is a per-site overload risk record nobody else holds. Drivers use it to price a job or refuse a site. It also makes each new representation stronger, because a pattern at one site beats one driver's word.

Sold at roughly £15 a month per vehicle, with a one-off £250 fee when the tool builds and sends a full challenge pack. Growth comes from the fine itself: a driver who gets a graduated fixed penalty tells the other drivers in the yard within a day, and haulier association forums and tipper Facebook groups carry it further. Owner-drivers renew because the next load is tomorrow.

Self-employed tipper, grab and skip lorry drivers in the UK who own one to five vehicles and haul for quarries, demolition contractors and waste transfer stations. They already pay for operator licence compliance, tachograph analysis and insurance, so a monthly tool fee sits in a budget line they recognise. The £250 challenge fee is set against a graduated fixed penalty that can run to £300 per axle plus a prohibition that stops the vehicle earning.

DVSA has been running targeted weight enforcement at roadside sites and publishes its enforcement approach and graduated fixed penalty rates on gov.uk. Its published policy names loaders and consignors, not only drivers, as people who can be prosecuted for overloading. At the same time cheap phone cameras, GPS and cloud storage make a per-load evidence record trivial to capture, which was not practical when the only proof was a paper ticket in a cab. Aggregate demolition and construction waste volumes keep tipper work busy, and material density varies enough that a visually normal load can be over.
