A posted kit of the exact cables, adapters and dock a UK software developer needs to rebuild a working setup after handing back the company laptop.

This is a physical product business, not software. You sell one box: a boxed, ready-to-plug bundle that turns a personal laptop into a full working machine. Inside are a powered USB-C dock tested against named laptop models, two display cables of the right generation, a UK plug power supply that actually delivers the wattage claimed, a wired keyboard, and a short printed card telling you which port to use first.

The idea borrows a move that works well in a very different trade. Motorbike parts sellers stopped selling generic chains and started selling exact-fit kits for one named bike, cut to length, with the right link. Buyers pay more because the guesswork is gone. Nobody does this for computer accessories, where the buyer is left to work out whether a dock supports two 4K screens at 60Hz on their specific chip.

The friction is real and dated. When a developer leaves a job in the UK, the employer takes back the laptop, the dock, the monitors and often the desk chair. What stays is a personal machine and a pile of mismatched cables. The person then has a fortnight of interviews and contract work where a broken screen setup costs them money.

The payer is a named group: UK software developers made redundant or moving between contracts, plus the growing number who go self-employed. Tech redundancies in the UK have been steady since 2023 and are documented in employer filings and public announcements. HMRC's off-payroll working rules, in force for large private-sector clients since April 2021, pushed many developers into working through their own limited company, where they buy their own equipment and can claim it as a business expense.

That expense point is the quiet commercial engine. A contractor buying through their own limited company treats the kit as an allowable business cost, so the real price they feel is lower than the sticker. You sell a £320 kit that costs a company director closer to £250 after corporation tax relief, and you say so plainly on the page with a link to HMRC's guidance on capital allowances for equipment.

Why it holds: the durable asset is a compatibility database you build yourself. Every kit sold is matched to a laptop model, a chip, an operating system version and a monitor pair. Returns and support emails tell you which combinations actually fail. Within a year you know that a specific dock silently drops to 30Hz on a specific MacBook running a specific version, and no chip maker or retailer publishes that. You use it to configure kits, to cut your own return rate, and to publish a compatibility checker that pulls buyers in from search.

Running it stays small. You hold roughly 40 stock lines, not 400. Fulfilment is one person picking boxes, or a third-party warehouse once volume justifies it. Distribution is where developers already are: sponsored posts in UK developer newsletters, a presence in redundancy support communities, and referral deals with the UK IT recruitment agencies who place these people and want them interview-ready.
