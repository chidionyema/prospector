ShiftLedger — the pay-slip checker built into the apps that already schedule your shifts.

Software that hospitality and warehouse agencies run for their own casual staff, showing each worker whether last week's pay matched the hours, breaks and holiday pay the law says they are owed.

A 22-year-old on a zero-hours contract in a bar, a warehouse or a hotel gets paid weekly and has almost no way to check the number is right. Rota apps like Deputy, Planday and RotaCloud already hold the shifts. Payroll holds the payment. Nobody joins the two and tells the worker, in plain words, what was owed.

ShiftLedger is software sold to the employer, not the worker. It plugs into the rota system and the payroll file, and reconciles them line by line. It checks the four things casual staff are most often short-changed on. Was every rostered hour paid? Was unpaid break time deducted correctly, or was time worked as an unpaid break? Was rolled-up holiday pay paid, at the 12.07% rate that became lawful again for irregular-hours workers for holiday years starting on or after 1 April 2024? And did the average hourly rate, after any deductions for uniform, transport or accommodation, stay above the National Minimum Wage band for that worker's age?

That last check is the one that sells it. HMRC enforces minimum wage, and the Department for Business and Trade publishes named-and-shamed employer lists; the December 2024 round named 524 employers owing about £16 million to roughly 172,000 workers. The single biggest cause in those rounds is deductions and unpaid working time, not a wrong headline rate. An operations director at a 40-site pub group is a solvent, motivated payer, because the fine is up to 200% of arrears, capped at £20,000 per worker, and the naming is public.

The worker side is what makes it grow. Every payroll run pushes each employee a plain-English breakdown in an app: here is what you worked, here is what you were paid, here is why they differ. Staff move constantly between employers in this sector. A worker who used it at one employer asks for it at the next, which turns the workforce itself into the sales channel — the pattern staff-benefit apps like Wagestream used to get into large employers.

This takes the widest possible view of the problem rather than picking one duty. It is not a minimum wage calculator, and it is not a payroll system. It sits between the rota and the payroll and audits the join, which is where the money actually goes missing.

The hard part to copy is the record. Every reconciliation ShiftLedger runs becomes a dated, signed audit trail of what was rostered, what was paid and what was corrected. When HMRC opens a minimum wage check, that history is the employer's defence, and it only exists if the software was running at the time. An employer two years in cannot switch to a rival without losing the evidence, and the pay data across thousands of shifts also builds a picture of which deduction and break practices actually trigger arrears — something no single employer can see alone.

Operations and HR directors at multi-site UK employers of casual staff: pub and restaurant groups with 20 to 200 sites, hotel groups, warehouse and fulfilment operators, and the recruitment agencies that supply them. Priced per worker per month, roughly £1 to £2, sold as insurance against a minimum wage arrears bill and a public naming.

Rolled-up holiday pay for irregular-hours and part-year workers became lawful again for holiday years starting on or after 1 April 2024, so thousands of employers changed how they pay holiday and many got the sums wrong. The Employment Rights Act 2025 brings guaranteed-hours offers and shift-notice duties for zero-hours workers, which means employers must track rostered versus worked hours far more carefully. HMRC's naming rounds keep growing, and rota software is now near-universal in hospitality, so the shift data finally exists in a machine-readable form.
