Runbook Rerun, proves your DR test to the auditor.

A fixed-fee service that runs your disaster-recovery failover test for real, then hands your operations manager the signed evidence pack auditors and insurers now demand.

Start from the raw pain, not from how anyone sells to it today. A UK operations manager at a 60-to-400-person software or fintech firm is asked, every year, to prove the business can keep running if its main cloud region dies. They do not have a spare weekend, a spare environment, or a spare engineer. So the test gets simulated on paper: a Word document describing what would happen, signed by someone who has never actually pulled the plug.

This service pulls the plug. For a fixed fee per exercise, we take the firm's own infrastructure-as-code and runbooks, stand up the failover in a second region, break the primary on purpose, time the recovery, and record every step. The deliverable is a dated evidence pack: actual recovery time, actual data-loss window, screenshots, command logs, the list of things that broke, and a re-test of the fixes. It is written to be handed straight to an auditor, an insurer, or an enterprise buyer's security questionnaire.

The recurring trigger is public and dated. Under the Bank of England, PRA and FCA operational resilience rules (PS21/3 and SS1/21), in-scope firms have been required since 31 March 2025 to stay within their set impact tolerances for important business services under severe but plausible scenarios, and to test that they can. The FCA has said firms must test regularly and evidence it. The DORA regime next door in the EU pushes the same expectation onto any UK software firm selling into European financial customers. And every enterprise security questionnaire a growing SaaS company answers asks for the date of the last DR test.

Why a service and not software. The chore is not tracking that a test is due, it is doing the test. Nobody inside a 90-person company wants to be the person who deliberately kills production on a Thursday. Selling the willingness to do the frightening thing, at a fixed price, with insurance behind it, is the product.

The growth motion is not cold outreach. Every exercise ends with an evidence pack that goes to a named auditor, insurer or enterprise buyer, with our name on it. Those readers see the same document across many clients and start asking for it. We also partner with the firms that already sit in this seat annually: ISO 27001 and SOC 2 auditors, cyber insurance brokers, and the cloud partners who resell architecture reviews. They cannot perform the test themselves without compromising their independence, which makes referring it the natural move.

The thing that compounds is the failure library. After a few hundred real failovers we hold something nobody else does: what actually breaks, by cloud, by stack, by company size, in Britain. Which DNS time-to-live setting silently doubles recovery time. Which managed database's promotion step takes eleven minutes when the vendor documents two. That library makes each exercise faster to run and lets us quote a recovery time before we start, which no first-time rival can. It also becomes the benchmark line in the report the auditor actually reads: how this firm's recovery compares with 200 similar ones.
