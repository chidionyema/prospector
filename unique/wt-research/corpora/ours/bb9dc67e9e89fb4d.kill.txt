Bench Ballast, weighted desk mats for shaky old flats.

A weighted, vibration-damping desk mat and monitor-arm base sold to developers working from bouncy Victorian floors, so their screens stop wobbling on every keystroke.

This is a physical product brand with one job: stop a desk from shaking. The core item is a 9 kg mineral-filled rubber mat that sits under a monitor arm clamp, plus a machined steel plate that spreads the clamp load across a thin, flexible desktop.

Two unrelated things collided to make this a real market. First, roughly a fifth of England's homes were built before 1919, and their suspended timber floors flex noticeably under load — the English Housing Survey records the age profile, and anyone who has walked across one knows the feeling. Second, home working became normal after 2020, and ONS labour market data shows hybrid working settled in rather than reversing. Developers then bought heavy kit for those homes: 34-inch ultrawides, dual 27-inch arms, and mechanical keyboards that hammer the desk. A cheap flat-pack desk on a springy floor plus a 10 kg screen on a lever arm gives you a monitor that bounces every time you type or someone upstairs moves.

The pain is daily and physical. People fix it badly today, with folded cardboard under the clamp, or a book, or by giving up and putting the screen back on its stand. There is no product sold for it, because the desk makers optimise for flat-pack shipping weight and the arm makers assume a solid 25 mm desktop.

Why this can hold value: the useful asset is measurement. Every buyer gets a free phone-based wobble test — record ten seconds of typing with the phone taped to the monitor, upload it, and get back the frequency and amplitude of the shake, plus which mat and plate combination kills it. That builds a growing library of real measurements matched to desk model, screen model, floor type and postcode-era housing stock. After a few thousand tests you can tell a buyer, before they spend anything, exactly what will happen — and you can design the next mat against the frequencies that actually show up, not guesses. Nobody selling desks or arms collects this, and no free tool exists to produce it.

Running it stays small. One contract manufacturer for the rubber mats, one UK laser-cutting shop for the steel plates, Shopify, and Royal Mail or a pallet courier for the heavy items. Sell at £89 for the mat, £39 for the plate, £119 as a set, against a landed cost near £30. Distribution is where the developers already are: r/battlestations, Hacker News "show your setup" threads, the mechanical keyboard community, and YouTube desk-setup channels who will happily film a before-and-after wobble test because the result is visible on camera.

Software developers and other screen-heavy home workers in older UK housing — flat-fronted Victorian terraces, converted flats, and 1930s semis with suspended timber floors. First buyers are the people already posting about monitor wobble in setup communities, then keyboard enthusiasts, then the audio and video editors who need a steady screen. Later, small UK software firms buying home-working kit for staff, where a £119 set is easier to approve than a new desk.

Hybrid working has settled rather than faded, so home desks are now permanent workstations rather than temporary ones — ONS labour market releases track the stable share of people working partly from home. At the same time ultrawide and dual-monitor arm setups have become normal and cheap, putting far more weight on a lever arm than desks were designed for. Most UK homes are old: the English Housing Survey shows a large share built before 1919, with suspended timber floors that flex. Those three facts together create a problem that did not exist when a single light screen sat on its own stand.
