PunctureFix — mobile bicycle roadside repair that gets delivery riders back on the road in 30 minutes.

A van comes to you during your shift and fixes your puncture or broken chain so you never lose a delivery hour.

This idea transports the model of instant roadside assistance from the car world to the bicycle world, focusing on gig-economy delivery riders who depend on their bikes to earn. When a rider gets a flat tyre or a snapped chain mid-shift, the nearest bike shop is often closed or too far away, and the AA’s bicycle cover only tows you home — you still lose the shift. PunctureFix stations a solo mobile mechanic in a van, carrying tubes, chains, basic tools, and a workbench, ready to roll to any rider within a defined city zone. The mechanic fixes the bike on the spot, typically in 20–30 minutes, letting the rider complete their remaining deliveries and keep that day’s income. Riders can pay per callout (£25) or sign up for a monthly subscription (£12.99) for unlimited callouts. The operator markets the service by leaving flyers at dark kitchens, posting in local rider Facebook groups, and giving stickers to riders for referrals. The why now is the explosive growth of app-based food and parcel delivery in UK cities — Royal Mail now hires couriers on bikes, Deliveroo and Uber Eats still dominate, and e-bike rental schemes are booming — all of which create thousands of daily riders who have no fast, field-based repair option. The acute pain is real: a single missed shift can cost £60-£100, so riders are highly motivated to pay a fraction of that to protect their earnings. The solo operator needs only a used van, a repair stand, and a small parts inventory; the service can launch with less than £1,000 of capital. While the core repair skill is not proprietary, the combination of route density, fast response, and membership habits can build a loyal local customer base that treats it like an insurance policy — once a rider has used it once, they’re likely to subscribe to avoid future loss. This is not a platform or marketplace; it’s a direct, mobile, low‑capital service that one person can run entirely alone, with bookings and dispatching handled by a simple Google Calendar and phone.

Gen-Z gig workers who do deliveries by bicycle or e-bike in dense UK cities (London, Manchester, Birmingham, etc.) — they pay out of their own pocket, either per callout or via a monthly membership.

The number of UK gig-economy delivery cyclists has grown sharply since 2020, and the trend is still rising as more restaurants offer delivery, grocery apps expand, and courier companies use freelance riders. None of these platforms provides mechanical support beyond putting you in touch with a shop. Meanwhile, roadside‑assist brands like the AA offer only bicycle recovery to a repair centre, not on‑the‑spot fixing, leaving a gap between total breakdown and no service at all. A solo operator can fill that gap quickly, at a price point that is trivial compared with lost shift income.
