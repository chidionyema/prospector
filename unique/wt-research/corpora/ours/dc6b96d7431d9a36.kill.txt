PermitLag, sells your unused chip-fab tool slot.

A booking tool that lets a US semiconductor startup resell its reserved but unused equipment time at a shared fab, priced the way airlines sell empty seats.

Small chip and photonics startups buy blocks of time on shared university and state-backed fabs. They pay for the block whether or not their wafers are ready. A run slips, a mask arrives late, a postdoc quits, and the slot burns.

This is a tool the startup's own hardware lead installs. It watches their internal run schedule and their fab's booking system. When a slot is going to go unused, it posts that slot to the other startups already accredited at that same fab, at a price that falls as the date gets closer.

This borrows the way airlines drop the price of an empty seat as departure nears, and applies it to cleanroom hours. Nobody prices fab time this way today. The fab's own booking page treats a slot as booked or free, with one price and no clock.

The payer is the hardware or process lead at a fabless or photonics startup with 5 to 40 staff, the person who signs the fab agreement. The trigger is recurring and predictable: every slipped tape-out, every failed lithography step, every month-end when the block expires.

The deliverable is money back on a slot that was going to be wasted, plus a filled slot for the buyer who needed one this week. The tool takes a cut of each transfer and a monthly seat fee from the seller.

The part that compounds is the record of who actually shows up. After a year, the tool knows which startups no-show, which slots reliably free up in the last 72 hours, and what a Tuesday night deep-etch slot at a given facility really clears for. New entrants cannot buy that history. It only exists by sitting inside the scheduling of both sides.

The public evidence that this pain is already paid for: the National Science Foundation's National Nanotechnology Coordinated Infrastructure lists published hourly rate cards for its member sites, and the CHIPS and Science Act (Public Law 117-167) funds the Natcast-operated National Semiconductor Technology Center, whose facility access is explicitly sold as paid time. New York's Albany NanoTech and California's Berkeley Marvell Nanolab both publish external user rates in dollars per hour. People are already writing these checks.
