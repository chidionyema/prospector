TestChest, rents phones your app-store reviewer will use.

A rentable rack of the exact old Android phones and iPhones UK app teams must test on, driven remotely, with every session recorded as evidence for store appeals.

Small UK software companies ship mobile apps and get rejected by Apple's App Review or Google Play's pre-launch checks on a device they do not own. The rejection names a model and an OS version. The team then buys a second-hand handset on eBay, or guesses.

TestChest is a physical rack of real handsets in a Manchester unit, wired to a browser. You pick the exact model and OS build named in your rejection notice, drive it live, and run your app. Each session records a video, the device logs, and a signed record of which build ran on which device at what time. That record is the thing you attach to your appeal or to your accessibility statement.

This borrows the way motor trade garages rent a diagnostic bay by the hour rather than every workshop buying the same rare tool. Nobody applies that to handsets, because the cloud device farms sold the subscription model instead.

The wedge is the physical estate plus the evidence record. Every rejection a customer uploads teaches the rack which model and OS pairs actually cause failures in the UK. After a year the rack holds a growing map of which specific handsets break which frameworks, and it buys stock accordingly. A rival starting fresh cannot buy that purchase history, and cannot buy 2019 handsets that are no longer made.

Growth comes from the rejection itself. The trigger is a store rejection email, which arrives constantly and unpredictably, so the entry point is a self-serve hourly rate with no contract. Retention comes from the Play Console target API level deadline each August, which forces every UK developer back onto old devices once a year.

This is the tech-vertical shape: it serves the Apple App Store and Google Play ecosystem, and the platform rule that hurts is Google Play's annual target API level requirement plus Apple's App Review Guidelines rejections, both published.

The mobile engineering lead or head of product at UK software companies of 5 to 60 people who ship a consumer app. They hold the tooling budget and are the person who receives the rejection email. Also UK digital agencies who build apps for clients and must prove the app works on the devices the client's customers actually hold.

Google Play publishes a target API level requirement with a fixed annual deadline, and apps that miss it stop being offered to new users. Apple publishes its App Review Guidelines and rejection reasons. Meanwhile UK public sector buyers increasingly ask suppliers to meet the Public Sector Bodies Accessibility Regulations 2018, which for mobile apps means testing on real assistive technology on real handsets, not a simulator.
