IHSS HourClaim – the primary carer's tool that builds your In-Home Supportive Services appeal packet, using the state's own rules to get the hours you're owed.

A web app that steps a California primary carer through a functional assessment of their loved one, then generates a ready‑to‑file IHSS fair hearing appeal packed with data‑backed arguments.

The tool turns a carer’s answers into a structured report that mirrors the IHSS functional index rankings. It automatically tailors the appeal arguments by matching the care recipient’s deficits against a proprietary database of past fair hearing decisions in California—showing what worked and what didn’t for similar cases. This borrows the way tax software optimises a return by comparing against audit outcomes, and applies it to a social‑care benefits battle. Because the California Department of Social Services keeps tightening assessments but cannot ethically build a challenger tool, every new restriction makes the tool more essential. The carer buys a fixed‑fee pack, not an ongoing subscription, keeping the commitment low and the value immediate. The evidence reports include plain‑English summaries of the relevant Welfare and Institutions Code sections and the All‑County Letters, so the packet is hearing‑ready.

Adult children or spouses caring for a parent with physical or cognitive impairments, typically in California, who are applying for or defending IHSS hours. They are time‑poor, often out‑of‑pocket, and the income from additional hours quickly covers the tool’s cost.

California’s Department of Social Services has been under pressure to reduce IHSS spending growth, leading to more restrictive interpretations of need and an increase in denied or reduced hours. At the same time, the state’s fair hearing system remains opaque: carers must navigate complex medical and legal arguments without accessible data on what persuades a judge. FOIA‑sourced results from hundreds of hearings now reveal patterns, making a data‑driven advocacy product possible.

People are actively seeking help with exactly this problem — one passage records a person asking for an IHSS provider denial hearing and for the hearing-division regulations and protocols [d2a7e6d78640b4ae], and multiple organizations have published dedicated guides and worksheets for challenging IHSS hour denials and preparing for fair hearings [f5b3b327369071f9, d8d7cea174b7710c, b8358be39504ecbf], which only exist because carers keep asking. The current help is free rather than paid, but demand met by free resources still shows the buyers and the need are real right now.

An attorney already markets paid help to California families pursuing IHSS appeals, showing this segment does hire and pay for appeal assistance, and winning hours converts directly into caregiver wages of roughly $16.38/hour, giving the payer both motive and a way for the tool's fixed fee to pay for itself. The caveat is that Disability Rights California represents appellants at no cost statewide, which may dampen — but does not eliminate — willingness to pay.

The passages state that in California only licensed attorneys may practice law or give legal advice, and that doing so without a license is a crime; the tool's core offering — individually tailored legal arguments and a ready-to-file appeal packet for a specific person's hearing — is legal advice delivered by a non-attorney business, which these passages say is prohibited. The passages do not carve out any exception for software or self-help products, so on this evidence the product's value cannot be delivered lawfully by an unlicensed provider.
