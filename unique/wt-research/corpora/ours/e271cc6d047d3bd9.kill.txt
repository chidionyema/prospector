SOC2 Evidence Drift, catches broken proof before audit.

A tool for startup founders in a SOC 2 Type II observation window that spots the day a control's evidence stops being produced, not the day the auditor finds out.

A SOC 2 Type II report covers a period, usually three to twelve months. The auditor does not test your controls today. They test whether each control operated continuously across every day of that window. Most founders learn a control broke on the day the auditor samples it, months later, when nothing can be fixed retroactively.

This tool watches the evidence itself, not the policy. It connects read-only to GitHub, AWS CloudTrail, Google Workspace and your ticketing tool. Then it learns the normal rhythm of each control. Access reviews land quarterly. Pull requests carry a second approver. Backup restore tests happen monthly. Vulnerability scans run weekly. When a rhythm stops, you get told within days, with the exact date range that will show up as a gap.

This borrows the way factory equipment monitoring works. Nobody waits for a machine to fail. They watch for the vibration changing and fix it during a planned stop. A control gap found on day 12 of a 365-day window is a two-hour fix. The same gap found in month eleven means restarting the observation period, which pushes an enterprise deal by a quarter.

It commits to shape (C), selling to technology companies. The buyer is the founder or head of engineering at a UK software company between 15 and 80 people, who has been sent a security questionnaire by a large customer and told a SOC 2 report is required to sign. The recurring trigger is the monthly moment the observation window advances and either accumulates clean evidence or quietly accumulates a gap.

The deliverable is a monthly gap report plus alerts, priced around £180 a month. It sits alongside the compliance platform, not against it.

The hard part it owns is the timeline of what actually happened inside one company's systems, day by day, reconstructed from logs the customer already has but never reads chronologically. That record compounds. By month nine it can show an auditor a continuous evidence trail nobody could rebuild after the fact. Ripping the tool out means losing the only chronological record of the window you are being judged on.

The public source proving this pain is already paid for is the AICPA's own SSAE 18 and Trust Services Criteria guidance, which states that a Type II opinion covers operating effectiveness throughout a specified period. UK firms selling into regulated buyers meet the same requirement, and the National Cyber Security Centre's Cyber Essentials Plus scheme runs on the same annual evidence logic. Firms already pay auditors five figures for the opinion, and pay again when a gap forces the window to restart.
