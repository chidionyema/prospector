Timing checks for chip startups selling to carmakers.

A test bench and signed report service that proves a startup's AI chip or module meets the automotive functional-safety timing numbers a Tier 1 buyer demands before design-win sign-off.

A small US fabless chip or edge-AI module company wins a design slot with a carmaker or a Tier 1 supplier like Bosch or Aptiv. The buyer then asks for something the startup cannot produce: measured evidence that the part detects its own faults inside the time the safety standard allows. That standard is ISO 26262, and the number is the fault-tolerant time interval. The startup has simulation results. The buyer wants bench measurements on real silicon, at temperature, with the fault deliberately injected.

This business sells the picks and shovels for that moment. It is a rented bench in Austin, Texas plus a fixed-fee report: fault-injection rigs, thermal chambers, clock-glitch and voltage-droop injectors, and a measurement harness that records how long each safety mechanism actually took to raise a flag. The deliverable is a diagnostic coverage and timing report the customer hands straight to their buyer's safety engineer, alongside the raw traces.

The angle is borrowed from aviation. Aircraft parts are not certified on the manufacturer's own word; an independent test house measures them, and the report travels with the part. Nobody does that routinely for AI silicon going into cars, so every startup builds a one-off rig, badly, under deadline.

The wedge is the data that piles up. After a hundred parts, this business knows which failure modes actually miss their timing budget on which process nodes, and which Tier 1 safety teams reject which evidence formats. That is a fault-and-rejection library no chip startup can build from one part, and no test-equipment vendor can build because they sell boxes and never see the verdicts.

Committing to shape B, tech-vertical: the ecosystem is automotive semiconductors and the deadline is the design-win safety review.

Growth comes from the Tier 1 side. Once two or three safety teams at large suppliers recognise the report format, they start naming it to every startup they evaluate, which turns the buyer's own review process into the sales channel.

The VP of engineering or head of silicon at a US fabless chip or edge-AI module company with 15 to 150 staff, chasing an automotive design win. Typical spend is $40,000 to $120,000 per part per cycle, and parts come back for re-test at every respin.

Carmakers are putting far more compute into vehicles for driver assistance, and NHTSA has been steadily expanding automatic emergency braking requirements, which pushes safety scrutiny down onto the chips. ISO 26262 second edition explicitly covers semiconductor parts. At the same time a wave of US AI-chip startups funded since 2023 is now reaching production silicon with no in-house safety test capability, because they hired architects and compiler engineers, not automotive validation staff.
