Console submission rejection fixes for indie game studios.

For indie game studios, this reverse-engineering service takes an Xbox, PlayStation, or Switch submission rejection and returns a fix list and resubmission plan.

When an indie studio submits a finished game to Microsoft's Xbox, Sony's PlayStation, or Nintendo's Switch, the platform runs it through a certification process (Microsoft's TCR, Sony's TRC, Nintendo's Lotcheck) and returns a pass or fail. Failures are reported against the platform's published technical rules but are not specific about what code, asset, or configuration broke. The studio pays the submission fee (Sony around $2,500 per submission; Microsoft and Nintendo charge similar), waits four to eight weeks for the result, and if rejected, fixes the problem and pays the fee again.

This is a TECH-VERTICAL picks-and-shovels service: it serves the console gaming ecosystem and the platform rule (TRC/TCR/Lotcheck), the per-submission fee, and the deadline each submission cycle imposes. The buyer is the Technical Director or CTO at an indie studio with five to fifty employees. Primary buyers sit in California (Los Angeles, San Francisco, San Diego, where Sony Interactive Entertainment is headquartered in Foster City) and Washington state (Seattle, Bellevue, where Microsoft Xbox and Nintendo of America are in Redmond), though the service operates nationwide.

The service reverse-engineers each rejection across many studios. Each failure is logged with the platform's reason code, the specific code, asset, or configuration that triggered it, the fix that worked, and how long the fix took. Over time the dataset compounds: a small set of reasons drives most submissions that fail (save data corruption on suspend or resume, HDR calibration, controller disconnect handling, accessibility settings, trophy persistence, network state on title update), and the fix patterns are stable across studios. The studio uploads the rejection report and gets back a structured fix plan within forty-eight hours, plus a pre-submission checklist to keep the same failure from recurring on the next title.

This borrows the way restaurant health inspections work in reverse: each violation is logged against a specific code (for example, 'Cold holding above 41°F' is code 3-501.16 in the FDA Food Code), the operator appeals with documented remediation, and the inspector verifies the fix on reinspection. Applied here, each console rejection has a specific TRC, TCR, or Lotcheck reference, the fix plan addresses that reference with specific code or asset changes, and the platform verifies the fix on resubmission.

The wedge is the proprietary data on failure patterns that compounds with each rejection logged. The platform's own docs explain the rules but not what specifically fails in practice. Manual QA consultancies address single studios but do not aggregate patterns across studios. The dataset grows only when many studios share their rejections through this service, so a single studio, consultancy, or platform cannot replicate the cross-studio view.

Indie game studios with five to fifty employees, whose Technical Director or CTO owns the certification process and whose CFO signs off on the per-submission budget. The studios sit mainly in California (Los Angeles, San Francisco) and Washington state (Seattle, Bellevue), with secondary clusters in Texas, New York, and Quebec. Per-submission budget is $1,500 to $3,000.

Microsoft tightened Xbox Series X|S certification in 2024 with new HDR, suspend and resume, and Quick Resume requirements. Sony updated TRC for PS5 in 2023 with new accessibility and trophy rules. Nintendo's Lotcheck process tightened for Switch OLED and the next Switch successor. Indie game releases on console grew double digits year over year through 2024 (Newzoo, ID@Xbox partner counts), and more studios are targeting multi-platform day-one launches, which multiplies the per-title rejection risk. The cost of a single failed submission is the platform fee ($500 to $2,500) plus four to eight weeks of delay, which translates into tens of thousands of dollars in lost or deferred revenue per title.
