SignedOff, the DPIA your NHS buyer's IG team will accept.

A tool for founders selling software to NHS trusts that turns your product into the data protection paperwork each trust's information governance team demands before purchase.

You sell software to NHS trusts. Before any trust will buy, its information governance team asks for a Data Protection Impact Assessment and a completed Data Security and Protection Toolkit position. Every trust asks in a slightly different order, on its own form, with its own reviewer. So you write the same substance eight times and lose six weeks per deal.

SignedOff is a tool you fill in once. You describe your product's data flows, hosting, sub-processors and retention in one place. It then produces the DPIA in the shape the specific trust in front of you uses, plus the answers to that trust's IG questionnaire, plus the Article 30 record and the sub-processor list their legal team asks for.

This borrows the way tax software works for one filing: you enter your affairs once, and the tool renders the return in the exact form each authority insists on. Nobody applies that to health service procurement paperwork, where the substance is identical and only the form differs.

The holding asset is a growing library of how real trusts actually review. Each time a founder using the tool gets a question back from an IG reviewer, that question, the trust, and the answer that cleared it go into the library. After a hundred deals you know that one trust always challenges data residency for sub-processors, and another always wants the retention schedule split by record type. That is a record of reviewer behaviour that no regulator publishes and no consultancy holds in usable form, because consultants work one client at a time and never see the pattern across trusts.

A solo person can run this. The public source material is open: NHS England publishes the Data Security and Protection Toolkit and its assertions, the ICO publishes DPIA guidance and the screening checklist under UK GDPR Article 35, and the NHS Digital Technology Assessment Criteria (DTAC) sets out exactly what a trust must check before buying digital products. The work is turning what the founder already knows about their own product into those documents, then learning from what comes back.

Money: a fixed fee per trust pack, around £600, and a yearly subscription around £2,400 for founders selling into many trusts at once. Distribution is owned, not bought: publish the reviewer patterns as short public notes about what a named trust's IG team actually asked, and founders in the same position will find them.

This commits to the sells-to-tech shape. The buyer role is the founder or head of commercial at a health software startup. The recurring trigger is each new trust deal reaching its information governance stage. The deliverable is the trust-shaped DPIA and DTAC pack. The public source proving the pain is already paid for is DTAC itself, which makes this assessment a mandatory step in NHS buying, plus the paid consultancy market that has grown around it.

Founders and commercial leads at UK health software startups selling to NHS trusts, typically firms of five to forty people with a signed pilot and no in-house privacy lawyer. They pay per trust deal, because the paperwork is what stands between them and the contract.

The NHS Digital Technology Assessment Criteria made a common assessment mandatory for digital products bought by NHS organisations, and the Data Security and Protection Toolkit now runs on a yearly submission cycle that trusts must meet. Every trust still runs its own local information governance review on top. UK GDPR Article 35 requires a Data Protection Impact Assessment for high-risk processing, which patient data almost always is. So the number of founders facing this exact gate has grown with every NHS digital buying push, while the review itself stayed local and inconsistent.
