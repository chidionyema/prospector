FirstGrant, your parent's Power of Attorney, guaranteed.

A platform that files a parent's Lasting Power of Attorney and only takes payment once the Office of the Public Guardian accepts it first time.

FirstGrant matches a family arranging a Lasting Power of Attorney for an ageing parent with a vetted solicitor or certificate provider from its panel, and only takes its fee once the Office of the Public Guardian (OPG) accepts the application. If OPG rejects it because of a mistake on the provider's side, the family pays nothing extra and FirstGrant refiles it free.

Getting an LPA wrong is common and the cost is time, not just money. A missing signature, the wrong witness, or a date out of order sends the whole £82 application back to the queue, and OPG's own published turnaround has stretched past four months. For a family watching a parent's memory decline, that delay can be the difference between registering in time and losing the window entirely, because once someone loses mental capacity, the only route left is the far slower and more expensive Court of Protection.

The Powers of Attorney Act 2023 gives OPG the power to move LPA registration online, and that rollout is running in phases through 2025 to 2027. For now, paper and digital rules sit side by side, with new identity-check steps that are still tripping people up. That overlap is exactly when rejection rates spike and families most need a service that carries the risk of a mistake instead of them.

Every rejected application that passes through FirstGrant gets logged against its exact reason for failing, building a dataset that no solicitor directory and no OPG guidance page holds in one place. That data trains a pre-submission check that keeps getting sharper at catching the specific errors that trigger a rejection right now, under the current rules, which is what turns a list of solicitors into a service that can credibly promise an outcome.

OPG is also the body deciding whether to accept the form, so it cannot ethically sell a guarantee against its own decision, or refund a family for its own delay, without putting itself on both sides of the transaction. It also has no reason to collect or publish granular rejection-cause data, because that isn't part of its job. That gap doesn't close whichever way OPG's process tightens next.

The adult child, usually between 35 and 55, who is organising a parent's affairs pays a fixed fee on top of OPG's own £82 registration fee. The decision is often made jointly with a partner or sibling, and it gets easy to justify once they've seen how much a rejected form costs in lost months, not just lost pounds.
