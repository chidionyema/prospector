KilnCheck — the annual kiln and glaze safety check that keeps UK pottery studios insured and open.

A visiting engineer service that tests, documents and certifies the kilns, extraction and glaze handling in small UK pottery and glass studios, so their insurer and landlord stay happy.

Small ceramics studios across the UK run electric kilns that draw 13kW or more, sit in railway arches and converted mills, and fire overnight while nobody is there. The owner is usually a maker with a City & Guilds in throwing, not electrics. Nobody services the kiln until it fails.

This business sends a trained engineer once a year. The visit covers four things: an electrical test of the kiln circuit and isolator to BS 7671, a check that the kiln's own thermocouples and controller cut out correctly, an airflow measurement on the extraction that removes fluoride and sulphur fumes during firing, and a swab test of work surfaces for lead and cadmium from glazes. The customer gets a dated certificate pack with photos, meter readings and a signed engineer's declaration.

The angle is borrowed from the gas industry. A landlord letting a flat in England and Wales must, under the Gas Safety (Installation and Use) Regulations 1998, hold a current CP12 certificate from a Gas Safe registered engineer. That one document created a whole national service trade with an annual, non-negotiable repeat visit. Kilns have no equivalent, yet they carry very similar risk: a fixed heat appliance in a rented space, fumes, and a fire load. This business creates that document voluntarily and then makes it required by the people who hold the purse strings.

That is the part that holds. The value does not sit in the inspection itself, which any competent electrician could learn. It sits in the relationship with the parties who demand the certificate. Studio insurance for makers in the UK is written by a small number of specialist brokers and schemes — including the well-known maker and craft schemes, and the cover many members buy through the Craft Potters Association and Craft Potters Charitable Trust. Arts landlords are similarly concentrated: ACME Studios, Bow Arts, SPACE, Yorkshire Artspace and Manchester's Rogue Artists' Studios between them house thousands of makers, and each has an estates manager who signs off on tenant appliances.

The founder's real work is signing perhaps fifteen of those organisations to a policy line that says: kilns must hold a current annual safety certificate. Once one insurer names the certificate in a policy schedule, every studio on that policy must buy it, every year, from whoever can actually do the visit. That relationship is what a rival cannot simply copy by buying a multimeter — it takes a year of estates managers' meetings and one insurer willing to move first.

Pricing: £220 for a single kiln, £160 for each additional kiln at the same address. A shared studio with eight kilns pays roughly £1,340 in one visit lasting half a day. Two engineers running four visits a week each, at an average of three kilns per site, bill around £11,000 a month. Costs are a van, a calibrated anemometer, a Megger tester and lead swab kits.

UK studio ceramics is dense enough to route efficiently. London, Bristol, Stoke-on-Trent, Manchester and Glasgow hold most of it. Start with one city, one insurer, one estates group.

The person paying is the studio manager or the maker who holds the lease — typically someone running a shared workshop of five to twenty benches in London, Bristol, Manchester, Stoke-on-Trent or Glasgow. They pay because their insurance renewal or their landlord's tenancy conditions ask for a current kiln certificate. Larger arts landlords such as ACME Studios, Bow Arts and Yorkshire Artspace may pay directly for a whole building of tenant kilns and recharge it through service charge.

Two changes make this land now. First, the Building Safety Act 2022 and the fire safety reforms that followed the Grenfell Inquiry have made landlords far more careful about what tenants plug in, and estates managers are now asking tenants to evidence appliance safety in writing. Second, energy costs since 2022 pushed many studios to fire overnight on cheaper tariffs, which means more unattended firings in buildings where nobody is present. Insurers have noticed. At the same time, HSE guidance on control of substances hazardous to health still treats lead-bearing glazes as a serious workplace exposure, and small studios almost never have any monitoring record to show if asked.
