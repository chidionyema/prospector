FailShare, tells chip startups whose foundry step broke.

A paid data service that shows fabless hardware startups how their wafer test failures compare with everyone else's at the same foundry node, so they know whether to fix their design or push back on the fab.

A hardware operations manager at a small fabless chip company gets a wafer test report back and cannot tell one thing: is this our design, or is it the fab having a bad week? The foundry will not say. TSMC, GlobalFoundries and Samsung Foundry all publish process yield claims to investors but never share per-lot defect patterns across customers, because every customer contract forbids it.

FailShare turns that silence into a business. Small fabless firms send us their anonymised wafer maps and electrical test summaries — the standard files their test house already produces. In return they get a quarterly read: at this node, at this fab, at this time, defects clustered at wafer edge ran 3x normal, and eleven other customers saw it too. Nobody sees anybody's design. Everyone sees the shape of the fab's month.

This flips the usual direction of information. The foundry is the party with power, and it holds the comparison data because it sits in the middle. It structurally cannot ever publish that comparison, because doing so would arm its own customers in price and credit negotiations against it, and would breach the confidentiality clauses it wrote itself. The more aggressively foundries push yield risk onto customers, the more valuable an independent pool becomes.

This is an AI-native shape in one narrow place: a model reads wafer map images and classifies defect signatures — edge ring, scratch, centre blob, random — which is work a yield engineer is paid roughly $130,000 a year to do by eye. We do not replace the engineer. We do the comparison across companies that no single engineer can ever see.

The pool compounds. Year one you have twenty companies and a thin read. Year three you have two hundred and you can date a fab's process excursion to the week. A late copier starts at zero and cannot buy the history.

We would base this in Austin, Texas, near the design community around Samsung Austin Semiconductor and the Texas Instruments ecosystem, and start with the companies who already meet at the same industry test conferences.
