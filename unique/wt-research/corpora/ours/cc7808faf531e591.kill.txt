Silent Steel — quiet mechanical keyboards sold with a printable noise report your office or client can accept.

A keyboard brand for developers in shared offices and on client video calls, where every board ships with a measured noise reading for the exact unit you bought.

This is a physical product business. You sell mechanical keyboards, built to be quiet, direct to software developers in the United Kingdom. What makes it different is what comes in the box with the board: a one-page noise report, measured on your actual unit before it shipped, with the decibel figure at typing distance.

The idea comes from fusing two unrelated changes. First, hybrid working made the office a shared, bookable space rather than a personal desk. Second, near enough every developer now spends hours a day on video calls, where a loud board is a live annoyance to other people rather than a private preference. Neither trend alone makes a business. Together they create a buyer who needs to prove a thing about their gear, not just enjoy it.

Why the report matters: many UK employers now run a display screen equipment assessment for home and hybrid workers, because the Health and Safety (Display Screen Equipment) Regulations 1992 require employers to assess workstations for users who work at a screen habitually, and that duty follows the worker home. Facilities and IT teams also set noise expectations for open-plan and hot-desk floors. A developer who wants the company to buy the board, or wants to bring their own board into a shared office, needs something to hand the person who says no. A named decibel figure on headed paper is that something. A generic Amazon listing cannot give it.

How it is made without a factory. You buy switch and case components, assemble and tune in-house — lubricated switches, foam and gasket damping, a tuned plate — and test each finished board in a small treated booth with a calibrated sound level meter. That is a two to six person operation: two assemblers, one on testing and dispatch, one running marketing and support. Assembly and tuning is the cost; the measurement is minutes.

The part that compounds is the measurement archive. Every board you ship adds a row: this switch, this lubricant, this foam stack, this plate, this measured result. After a few thousand boards you know which build produces which number, so you can promise a figure before you build it, and you can quote a real number in your listings while rivals write "quiet". Nobody else holds per-unit measured data on their own builds, because nobody else measures every unit. That is the asset, and it is why the price holds above a commodity board.

Sell at roughly £180 to £280 a board, direct through your own shop, with a business invoice option and a purchase order route, because the real order is often placed by an IT manager buying six at once. Route to customers: developer newsletters, Hacker News and Lobsters, mechanical keyboard communities, and — the one that actually scales — the office managers and IT buyers who reorder. Add replacement switch packs and a re-tune service to bring people back.
