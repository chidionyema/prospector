PSTI Rig — a plug-in test board that proves your smart gadget meets the UK's new default-password ban before you ship it.

A pre-built hardware test rig and firmware test suite that lets a small IoT hardware team prove their product meets the UK's connectable-device security law before a retailer will list it.

Since 29 April 2024, any connected consumer product sold in the UK — smart plugs, baby monitors, doorbells, fitness trackers — has had to meet the security rules in the Product Security and Telecommunications Infrastructure Act 2024 and its accompanying Product Security and Telecommunications Infrastructure (Security Requirements for Relevant Connectable Products) Regulations 2023, enforced by the Office for Product Safety and Standards (OPSS). Schedule 1 of those regulations bans universal default passwords, requires a public vulnerability disclosure contact, and requires the manufacturer to state a minimum security update period. A small hardware startup's firmware engineer has to prove all three, in writing, often before a retailer like Amazon UK, Currys or John Lewis will even agree to list the product, and before UKCA-style self-certification paperwork can be signed off.

PSTI Rig sells, as a physical product through a normal Shopify/Amazon UK storefront, a small USB-connected test board pre-loaded with a firmware harness that plugs into the developer's own device over its debug or provisioning interface. It runs a fixed battery of checks mapped line-by-line to Schedule 1: it hunts for hardcoded or sequential default credentials, checks that password-reset flows enforce per-device uniqueness, probes for an accessible update mechanism, and confirms a vulnerability-disclosure endpoint responds. It spits out a signed PDF report the founder can hand to a retailer's compliance team or keep on file for OPSS. A companion subscription (a small recurring add-on to the hardware sale) pushes new test signatures every time OPSS updates its guidance or a new CVE class shows up in consumer IoT.

This is not a compliance dashboard or a filing tool — OPSS already publishes the checklist for free, as a PDF. What it doesn't do, and structurally can't do as a regulator, is sell or maintain physical test hardware calibrated against real embedded debug interfaces (UART, JTAG, BLE GATT, Matter commissioning flows), or keep that harness current as attack techniques evolve. A generic ESP32 or Raspberry Pi dev board doesn't ship with that harness either — building the actual bench of protocol-aware attack scripts against real hardware interfaces is exactly the kind of a firmware engineer would rather buy than write. The moat is the accumulating library of device-interface attack scripts and default-credential dictionaries, calibrated against real production silicon (Espressif, Nordic, Realtek chipsets) — that gets harder to replicate with every product generation it's tested against, and once it's wired into a startup's hardware test bench and CI pipeline, ripping it out means re-deriving the whole test suite from scratch.

The firmware or embedded software engineer — often also the technical co-founder — at a small UK IoT hardware company of roughly 2 to 20 people, building a smart plug, camera, monitor, or similar connected consumer device, who has to sign off that the product meets PSTI before a retailer will stock it or before launch.

PSTI's security requirements became legally enforceable on 29 April 2024, with OPSS able to fine manufacturers up to £10 million or 4% of global turnover for non-compliant connectable products sold in the UK. Retailers have started asking suppliers for compliance evidence as a condition of listing, and small hardware teams who previously ignored this because 'it's just a checklist' are now being asked for proof they can't produce from a PDF alone.
