Freight Clause, prices the AI clauses in client contracts.

A pricing tool for small agency owners that reads the AI and IP clauses in a client's contract and tells you what delivering them will actually cost you.

An agency owner signs a client contract, and buried in the schedule is a line saying no generative AI may be used in any deliverable, or that all AI use must be disclosed and logged, or that the agency indemnifies the client against any AI-related IP claim. The owner signs it because the retainer is worth £8,000 a month. Nobody prices what that clause costs to honour.

This borrows the way freight forwarders quote a shipment. A forwarder does not quote a flat rate. They read the routing, spot the surcharges — the low-sulphur fuel levy, the congestion fee, the customs hold — and hand you a landed cost per line before you commit. Freight Clause does the same for a contract schedule. You upload the client's terms. It returns a per-clause cost sheet: this disclosure duty adds roughly 3 hours a month of logging per working designer, this no-AI warranty means your writers cannot use the tools your rate card assumed, this indemnity has no cap and no insurance behind it.

The deliverable is a one-page priced schedule the owner takes into the negotiation, plus suggested redlines with a substitute clause that keeps the client comfortable and the agency solvent. It sells at £250 per contract reviewed, or £180 a month for unlimited reviews for agencies signing several a quarter.

Why it holds: the tool compounds a library of real UK agency contracts and what each clause actually cost to deliver, measured after the fact. Owners come back at renewal and say the logging duty took four hours, not three. That feedback loop is a data asset a template shop or a law firm cannot build, because neither one is in the room when the work gets delivered.

This is the SELLS-TO-TECH shape. The buyer is the owner or operations director of a UK digital, design or marketing agency of 5 to 40 staff. The recurring trigger is signing or renewing a client master services agreement. The public source proving the pain is already paid for is the UK government's own procurement position: the Crown Commercial Service's Digital Outcomes and Specialists framework terms, and the Cabinet Office's Procurement Act 2023 guidance on transparency notices, both require suppliers to declare and evidence their use of automated tools on public-sector work. Agencies on those frameworks already pay solicitors hourly to interpret those schedules. Private clients have copied the language wholesale, so the same duty now lands on agencies with no public work at all.
