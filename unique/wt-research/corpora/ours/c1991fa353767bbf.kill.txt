MotorMate — the mobile pre-MOT check that meets private hire drivers at the taxi rank and fixes the fails before the test.

A mobile mechanic who meets minicab and taxi drivers where they wait, checks the exact items a council licensing test fails on, and fixes the cheap ones on the spot for a flat £45.

This is one person with a van, a tyre depth gauge, a brake tester's checklist, a headlight aim board and a box of bulbs, wipers, washer jets and number plate screws. You park at the private hire rank, the airport feeder lot, or the office car park of a big operator, and you check drivers' cars against the licensing test they actually have to pass.

Here is the thing outsiders miss. A private hire driver in the UK does not just take a normal MOT. Most licensing councils run their own vehicle test on top, usually twice a year, and it is harsher than an MOT. Transport for London's private hire vehicle licensing inspection, run at approved sites, checks things a standard MOT ignores — bodywork condition, seat and trim damage, working interior lights, tyre tread above the council's own minimum rather than the legal 1.6mm. Councils like Birmingham, Manchester and Wolverhampton publish similar vehicle condition standards for hackney carriage and private hire licensing.

That gap is the whole business. A driver passes an MOT and then fails a council licence test on a torn seat, a hazy headlight, a chipped windscreen in the wiper sweep, or tread at 2.0mm when the council demands 3.0mm. A fail means a re-test fee, a second trip, and — this is the part that hurts — days off the road with no earnings while the plate is suspended.

This borrows the way a driving instructor sells a mock test before the real one. Nobody sells the mock test for private hire vehicles. So you do: a 25-minute inspection using the council's own published checklist, a photo of every item, and a plain sheet saying pass, fix now, or book the garage. Bulbs, wipers, washer jets, plate screws, number plate lamps, boot and interior lights, hazard warning triangle checks and tyre pressure get done there and then at cost plus a small markup. Anything bigger you photograph and refer.

What keeps this yours is not the spanner work. It is the record. After six months of testing the same drivers at the same rank, you know which specific fail item the local licensing office has been picking up on this quarter, because you have seen fifty cars come back from it. That is a pattern only the person standing at the rank collects. The council does not publish it. The garage sees one car at a time and never connects them.

Run it as a £45 flat check, £25 for a re-check before the test, and a £15 monthly "plate protection" that gets you a check the week before every licence renewal date. You hold the renewal dates, so you book yourself. Distribution costs nothing: you stand where drivers already queue, and one WhatsApp group per rank does the rest.

Councils have been tightening private hire vehicle standards since the Department for Transport issued its Statutory Taxi and Private Hire Vehicle Standards in July 2020, which pushed licensing authorities to apply firmer and more consistent checks. Wolverhampton's out-of-area licensing boom means tens of thousands of drivers now hold a plate from one council while working in another city, so they face a test they cannot easily attend or prepare for. At the same time, second-hand car and tyre prices have stayed high since 2022, so drivers are running vehicles longer and closer to the condition limits. A fail costs more than it used to, and there are more marginal cars on the road than there were five years ago.
