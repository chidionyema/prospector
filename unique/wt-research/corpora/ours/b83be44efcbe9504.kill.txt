NightWatch Kit — the thermal blackout blind that lets a shift worker sleep through daylight.

A made-to-measure blackout blind kit, sold direct to NHS and emergency-service shift workers, that blocks daylight and heat in a rented or mortgaged bedroom without drilling.

This is a small direct-to-consumer product business selling one thing well: a genuinely lightless bedroom blind, cut to the buyer's window size, fitted without tools or holes.

The idea borrows from a different sector entirely. Photographic darkrooms and hospital sleep laboratories have solved total light exclusion for decades using magnetic edge seals and light-trap side channels. Domestic blind makers have never bothered, because domestic buyers ask for "blackout" and accept the glowing halo around the edges. A night-shift nurse cannot accept it.

The buyer is specific. Around 27% of NHS staff work night or rotating shifts, and NHS England's own health-and-wellbeing guidance for shift workers tells staff to make the bedroom dark and cool. It tells them to do it; it does not sell them the thing that does it. Police, ambulance, prison and fire service staff have the same problem and the same advice.

The product is a rigid side-channel system in a powder-coated aluminium track, with magnetic or high-tack removable fixings that satisfy a tenancy agreement's no-drilling clause and a landlord's deposit deduction rules. The buyer measures with a printed guide, the blind is cut to that measurement, and it arrives ready to press into place. Add a summer version with a reflective backing, because a top-floor flat under a July roof defeats sleep as surely as light does.

Pricing sits around £160 to £320 a window, against a £40 supermarket blackout roller that leaks light down both sides. The margin comes from made-to-measure: nothing is held in stock, each unit is cut to order by a fabricator, and there is no returns pile of odd sizes.

What holds it is the route to the buyer, not the fabric. NHS trusts run staff benefit schemes through providers like Vivup and Health Service Discounts, and the Blue Light Card covers roughly 3 million emergency service, NHS and social care members. Getting listed inside those schemes is a slow, relationship-led process with a real approval queue. A generic blind retailer cannot be listed as the shift-sleep product, because the listing is granted to a supplier whose whole catalogue is aimed at that member group. That channel is captive, it is renewed annually, and it puts the product in front of the exact person whose employer has just told them to darken their bedroom.

Operationally this is two to five people: one who owns the trust and scheme relationships, one who runs the measurement-to-fabrication workflow, and an outsourced cut-to-size fabricator paid per unit.

NHS nurses, paramedics, radiographers and porters on rotating night shifts, plus police and prison officers, buying for their own bedroom. Typical order is one or two windows at £160 to £320 each, often paid through an employer salary-sacrifice or staff-discount scheme rather than out of the current account.

NHS England's health and wellbeing framework and its published shift-worker guidance now actively tell staff to control light and temperature in the bedroom for sleep recovery, which turns a vague comfort issue into named advice from the employer. At the same time, retrofitted loft conversions and modern insulation have made top-floor bedrooms hotter in summer, and the Blue Light Card and Vivup schemes have matured into a reliable way to reach roughly 3 million shift workers with a single listing.
