Repeat Rate — the tool that tells a freelancer which clients will still be paying in six months, and which are already gone.

A free tool for freelance designers, developers and writers that reads your own invoice history and warns you which clients are quietly winding down, months before the work stops.

Start from the raw pain, not from how anyone solves it today. A freelancer's income does not fall off a cliff. It thins. A client who paid £3,200 a month for a year drops to £2,100, then £1,400, then goes quiet in August and never comes back. The freelancer notices in October, when rent is due and the pipeline is empty. Nobody sees the decline while it is happening, because a single invoice always looks fine on its own.

Repeat Rate is a free tool you connect to the invoicing you already use. It reads FreeAgent, Xero, QuickBooks or a Stripe account through their official connections, all of which are open to third-party apps in the UK. It ignores your profit and loss entirely. It only looks at the shape of each client relationship over time: how much they pay, how often, how long they take, and whether the gaps between jobs are getting longer.

Then it does one thing. Every Monday it shows you a short list: clients drifting away, clients holding steady, clients growing. Each one carries a plain reason. "Meridian Design last briefed you 47 days ago. Their normal gap is 21 days. Their last three invoices fell from £2,800 to £1,950 to £900." That is the whole free product, and it is genuinely useful on its own, which is why people will keep opening it.

The money comes from what only this tool can see. Once thousands of UK freelancers connect their invoice histories, the tool holds something nobody else has: what actually happens next after a client starts drifting. It learns that in UK design and development work, a client whose gap doubles and whose invoice value drops by a third comes back only about one time in five, and that the ones who do come back nearly always do so after a specific kind of contact. That pattern cannot be read from any one freelancer's books. It only exists across many.

So the paid tier, around £16 a month, tells you the odds and the move. It ranks your at-risk clients by how much money is realistically recoverable, drafts the specific check-in that has historically preceded a returning client, and tells you how many weeks of income you are on track to lose so you can start replacing it now rather than in October. It also forecasts your next three months from your real client behaviour, not from an optimistic guess, which is the number a freelancer actually needs when deciding whether to take a badly-paid job.

It spreads by itself. The free weekly list is the kind of thing freelancers screenshot and post, because "this told me my biggest client was leaving six weeks before they told me" is a story people share. It also has a natural forcing function: the tool asks you to invite the accountant or bookkeeper you already work with to see your forecast, and UK freelance-focused accountants have hundreds of clients each.

It fits UK rules cleanly. Reading your own accounting data with your permission through an official connection is ordinary use of these platforms' published interfaces. You are the data controller for your own records; the tool processes on your behalf under UK GDPR, and the patterns it learns across users are aggregated and stripped of any client identity before they ever inform anyone else's forecast. It gives no regulated financial advice, arranges no credit, and touches nobody's money.

UK freelance designers, developers, writers and consultants billing roughly £40,000 to £120,000 a year through three to fifteen recurring clients. They pay around £16 a month, personally, on a card, because losing one £2,500-a-month client costs more in a single month than the tool costs in twelve years. Freelance-focused accountancy practices are the second payer, at a per-seat rate, because a client who sees a lean quarter coming three months out is a client who does not miss a payment on account.

Two things changed. Open banking and open accounting connections in the UK now let a small tool read a freelancer's invoice history properly, with permission, through published interfaces that FreeAgent, Xero and QuickBooks all maintain for third-party apps. Ten years ago this needed a spreadsheet export and manual work. Separately, HMRC's move to quarterly digital updates for self-employed people means freelancers are being pushed to keep their records current and connected all year rather than in one panic each January. That makes the underlying data live rather than stale, and it puts a freelancer in front of their own numbers four times a year instead of once.
