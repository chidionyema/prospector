MicroDose Mobile — the pharmacy that comes to your care home and packs every resident's weekly medicines.

A small chain of van-based dispensing hubs that packs and delivers weekly blister trays to independent care homes and supported-living houses in one town, with a signed round done in person.

Start with the raw problem, not the industry. A 22-bed independent care home in one English town gives out roughly 900 tablet doses a week. Someone has to order them, check them against the doctor's list, pack them into day-by-day trays, and record every single one. Today a high-street chemist does this in the back of a shop between serving walk-in customers, and the home's staff spend hours chasing missing items on a Friday afternoon.

This business does one thing only. Each unit is a van and a pharmacist working from a small licensed room, serving a fixed round of 8 to 12 homes inside a 20-minute drive. No shop front, no walk-in trade, no perfume aisle. The pharmacist visits every home on the round each week, packs the trays, resolves the mismatches face to face with the home manager, and takes the signature there and then. A second town gets a second van and a second pharmacist. That is the whole chain.

Why it holds. Each round is a licensed pharmacy premises registered with the General Pharmaceutical Council, and NHS dispensing needs an NHS England contract for that premises. Getting a new pharmacy contract in England is not open — since the 2013 regulations you generally need to win it through the Pharmaceutical Needs Assessment your local Health and Wellbeing Board publishes, or buy an existing contract. That is a genuine legal gate. A software company or a national delivery firm cannot copy this by writing code; they would need the same contract, and the contracts are finite. In Scotland and Wales the equivalent route runs through the health board, so the same gate applies with different paperwork.

The money comes from two places. NHS England pays a dispensing fee and a monthly payment per prescription item under the Community Pharmacy Contractual Framework, which is the bulk of it. Then the home pays a modest monthly service charge, typically £40 to £90 per resident, for the weekly on-site visit, the medicines audit trail, and the paperwork the Care Quality Commission asks for at inspection. That second line is what makes it a proper business rather than a squeezed NHS margin.

One pharmacist plus one technician can serve about 250 residents. At that scale the round bills roughly £14,000 to £20,000 a month before medicine cost, and the van, rent on a small room, and two salaries come out of that. Three rounds is a real business run by two or three people in the office. Twenty rounds would be a national chain, and that is not the plan.

The route to customers is a person in a car. There are around 15,000 care homes for older people in England and thousands more supported-living houses, most owned by families or small groups running one to five sites. The buyer is the owner or the registered manager, and they are reachable. CQC publishes every home, its rating, its bed count and its address as an open dataset. You drive to the ones with 15 to 40 beds inside your radius and ask who does their medicines and whether Friday afternoons are a problem. Homes switch pharmacy when a medicine error shows up in an inspection report, and those reports are public, so you know exactly who is unhappy and when.

Two payers. NHS England pays the dispensing fees and item payments for every prescription filled, under the community pharmacy contract. The care home itself pays a monthly service charge per resident, roughly £40 to £90, for the weekly visit, the tray packing and the medicines records. The buyer who signs is the registered manager or owner of an independent care home with 15 to 40 beds, or the operator of a small group of supported-living houses.

Independent care homes are losing their local chemist. Community pharmacy closures in England have run at a high rate since 2016, and the ones that remain are cutting back on the unpaid extras like care home rounds because dispensing margins are thin. At the same time CQC inspection reports keep flagging medicines management as the reason a home drops a rating, so managers are under real pressure to fix it. That combination — the old supplier walking away while the inspection risk rises — is what makes a home willing to pay a service charge on top of the NHS work. It also means existing pharmacy contracts come up for sale regularly, which is the cheapest way into the licence.
