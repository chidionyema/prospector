Ratchet & Pawl — desk kit that turns a developer's own laptop into the dedicated build machine for a client.

A physical kit sold to freelance developers and small dev shops so an old laptop becomes a locked-down, client-dedicated build and CI box on their desk.

This is a boxed product, not software. Each kit contains a slim aluminium under-desk mount, a USB-C power delivery brick rated for continuous duty, a managed gigabit switch with a labelled patch lead, a hardware kill switch, and a pre-imaged bootable SSD in a caddy. You clip an old laptop under your desk, boot from the SSD, and it becomes a dedicated build runner tied to one client's repository.

The idea fuses two changes that nobody has put together. First, cloud CI bills have moved from trivial to painful for small teams. GitHub-hosted runner minutes and equivalent services are now a real line item for a two-person shop running long test suites. Second, the UK now has a statute that makes client data location a paid conversation. The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 amends the UK GDPR regime, and clients in legal, health and public sector work increasingly write data residency clauses into contracts. A freelancer who can say the client's code and test data never left a machine in their own office, on their own switch, with a physical power cut-off, is answering a question buyers now actually ask.

The business sells the kit at around £280 and a consumable refresh at around £45 a quarter. The refresh is the real engine: a new imaged SSD each quarter, with the base image already carrying the current long-term-support Linux kernel, the current Node and Python toolchains, and a signed manifest listing exactly what is on it. That manifest is the thing a freelancer staples to an invoice or hands to a client's information security reviewer.

The compounding asset is the manifest archive. Every image ever shipped is retained with its hash and its full package list. When a supply chain problem surfaces months later, the brand can tell every customer, by serial number, whether their box ever ran the affected version. No cloud CI vendor can do that for hardware sitting under someone's desk, and no hardware vendor holds the software bill of materials. The brand holds both because it ships both.

Operations stay small. Assembly is kitting bought-in parts, imaging is scripted, and fulfilment is one person picking boxes two days a week. Gross margin on the kit is roughly 45%, and on the quarterly refresh roughly 70%.
