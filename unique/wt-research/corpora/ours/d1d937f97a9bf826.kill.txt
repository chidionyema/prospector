Car Data Liberation Agent.

A solo-operated service that extracts, normalises, and enforces access to vehicle telematics data under the EU Data Act for independent repairers and usage-based insurers, handling manufacturer resistance as a permanent service retainer.

Broadening the pain from 'compliance tool' to 'adversarial liberation service' reveals that independent aftermarket players (repair shops, UBI insurers) have a legal right to car data but lack the technical and legal muscle to force compliant access from OEMs who actively throttle, restrict, or delay. A solo operator can build a productized service combining: (a) a technical extraction agent (one-click connector to OEM APIs with fallback to physical OBD-II dongles where APIs are gamed), (b) a legal enforcement layer (automated dispute letters, regulatory complaints to national Data Act authorities, and pre-negotiated data-sharing agreements), and (c) a data normalization pipeline (converting raw telematics into actionable formats for each client's software). The service is sold as an annual retainer ($500–$1,500/shop per year, $5,000–$15,000/insurer per year) covering unlimited access requests and dispute handling. The solo operator builds a library of OEM-specific access patterns and legal precedents — proprietary knowledge that compounds with each dispute won. No OEM will ever build this, and traditional compliance SaaS vendors lack the adversarial posture to push back on manufacturers. The payer is the independent repairer or UBI insurer who now has a legal right but no practical way to exercise it.

Independent repair shops in EU (10–50 vehicles serviced per week, annual revenue €300k–€1M) and usage-based insurance startups (3–20 employees, pre-revenue to €5M ARR) that need telematics data to offer competitive products but cannot afford legal teams or custom engineering for each OEM.

The EU Data Act entered application 12 September 2025 — the first access disputes are appearing, creating a vacuum where no established service exists to help data rightsholders enforce their rights. OEMs are actively resisting, and the window to build proprietary enforcement playbooks is open now before law firms or big compliance consultancies enter.
