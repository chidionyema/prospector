PensionSplit, cuts your child benefit tax charge legally.

A tool for parents earning £60,000–£80,000 that calculates the exact pension or gift-aid payment needed to claw back their High Income Child Benefit Charge, then produces the evidence HMRC accepts.

If you earn over £60,000 and someone in your house claims Child Benefit, HMRC takes some of it back through your tax return. That is the High Income Child Benefit Charge. It is fully clawed back at £80,000. The rule is written on 'adjusted net income', not gross salary — and adjusted net income is a figure you can legally reduce, after the tax year has ended, by paying into a pension or making a Gift Aid donation.

This tool asks for the numbers on your payslips, your P11D benefits in kind, your savings interest and your rental or dividend income. It then works out the precise payment that pulls you under the threshold, and shows what that payment is worth: the Child Benefit you keep, plus higher-rate relief, plus the removal of the 60%-plus effective rate band you are sitting in. For a family with three children the combined effect can exceed the payment itself.

The angle here is inversion. Most tools built around a tax rule help you comply with it. This one grows in value precisely as HMRC pushes harder — and HMRC has been pushing hard. It cannot ship this itself. HMRC's own calculator tells you what you owe. It is structurally barred from telling you how to arrange your affairs so you owe less, because that is tax planning advice on a commercial side, and a tax authority cannot take a side against its own collection.

What compounds is the calculation library, not the arithmetic. Every case teaches the tool which income items people forget: salary sacrifice interactions, the £1,000 property allowance, bond gains with top-slicing, and the carry-forward of unused pension annual allowance from the previous three years. The output is a dated worksheet with the legislative references and the payment receipts attached, so the figure on the tax return can be defended if it is queried.

It sells at £120 for one year, or £180 including the previous four tax years, which can still be amended. It is a one-person business: the model is the product, and the support load is email.

Parents aged 35-55 in dual-income households where one person earns between £60,000 and £80,000 and Child Benefit is claimed. Around 700,000 people fall in this band. They are typically NHS consultants, senior teachers on leadership pay, police inspectors, and mid-level private sector managers — people who file a Self Assessment return only because of this charge and who have no accountant. A second buyer is the small independent financial adviser who wants the worksheet to hand to a client without doing the modelling.

The threshold moved from £50,000 to £60,000 in April 2024 and the taper now runs to £80,000, which pulled in a fresh population of higher earners who had never filed a return. HMRC has been issuing discovery assessments and penalties to people who did not know the charge applied to them, and the tribunals have heard a steady stream of these cases. Pay rises across the NHS, teaching and policing since 2023 have pushed a large cohort over £60,000 for the first time. Meanwhile pension annual allowance carry-forward remains available for the prior three years, so the window to act on old years is open but closing on a rolling basis.
