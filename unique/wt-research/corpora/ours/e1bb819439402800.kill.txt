BedBridge — the family carer's broker for the hospital bed your relative is blocking.

When a hospital wants your relative out, BedBridge sells that discharge slot to the care provider who gains most, and pays the family a share.

Here is the plain version. An NHS trust in England is fined nothing but loses money every day a medically fit patient stays in a bed. Since April 2023 the trust itself pays for the first stretch of that patient's post-hospital care under the discharge fund. The family, meanwhile, is pushed to accept whatever bed the discharge team names, usually in a hurry, often far away, often at a price the family then funds for years.

BedBridge sits on the family's side of that table and runs a short, timed sale. When a relative is declared ready to leave, the family signs up. BedBridge then runs a 48-hour closed bid among care homes, home care agencies and reablement providers within a radius the family sets. Providers bid on two things: the price they will hold for the first 12 weeks, and the date they can take the person. BedBridge takes a slice of the difference between the discharge team's default placement price and the winning bid, plus a fixed fee from the winning provider for the confirmed admission.

Why this works only now. The Health and Care Act 2022 and the discharge funding that followed made speed worth real money to the trust, and made the first weeks of care a purchase the system is desperate to complete. Beds sitting empty in a private care home are pure loss; a filled bed on a Friday is worth more than a slightly better price on a Tuesday. That gap between what an empty bed is worth to a provider and what a rushed family pays is the whole business.

The angle is deliberately awkward for the people imposing the rush. The NHS and the council both have a duty to arrange discharge, and both publish free guidance about it. Neither can ever run this: a trust cannot take a commercial side against its own placement budget, and a council cannot auction a placement it is statutorily required to arrange on a best-interests basis. The value grows precisely as discharge pressure grows — the harder the system pushes, the more a provider will pay for a guaranteed same-week admission and the more a family stands to save.

The thing that compounds is the bid history. After a few thousand auctions, BedBridge knows what each named home actually accepts on a Thursday in February versus what its rate card says. No provider sees another's bids. No council publishes them. That price book is the reason a late copier cannot start level, and it is the reason families come back for the next relative.

Running it is mostly software. Bids come in through a form, the radius and the ranking are computed, the family sees three options with real names and real dates. A human only joins for the contract signature and the CQC rating check.

Two payers. Care homes, home care agencies and reablement providers in England pay a fixed fee per confirmed admission won through the auction — the ones with empty beds and a sales team already chasing discharge coordinators. And the family pays a share of the saving against the discharge team's default placement price, capped, and only if they accept a bid.
