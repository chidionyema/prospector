PeakPence — the dashboard that tells you the highest-earning delivery windows in your postcode, built from real rider data.

PeakPence shows UK delivery riders when and where every app pays most, so you choose shifts that earn you the maximum per hour.

Delivery riders running multiple apps face a constant decision: which zone, which time slot, which platform will pay best? Platforms show boost areas in real time, but never show a predictive view across all platforms a rider could work for. PeakPence is a vertical SaaS dashboard that riders check before they start their shift. It blends anonymised earnings data from thousands of riders across Deliveroo, Uber Eats, Just Eat and Stuart to produce postcode-level predictions of hourly earnings by day and time window, updated daily.

This borrows the yield‑per‑hour optimisation airlines use to price every seat on a flight, applying it to a person’s own time. By pooling data across many riders, the tool surface patterns no single platform will ever reveal — for example, that taking Stuart orders in EC1 between 6pm and 8pm pays 22% above the city average on a rainy Thursday. The rider sees a simple map with colour‑coded windows and a suggested best next block to work.

Why it holds: once a critical mass of riders in a city share their earnings feeds, the predictions become materially better than anything a rider could guess or scrape from a single app. That data compounds; a rival starting from scratch would face a cold‑start gap in every new city. The deepest moat is the proprietary dataset itself — granular, real‑world pay‑by‑geography‑by‑time‑by‑weather data that no platform and no public body collects.

A UK delivery gig worker aged 18–27, typically juggling two or three apps on a bicycle, scooter or car. They feel acute time‑poverty and income volatility, and they already pay for phone data, vehicle maintenance and insulated bags. A monthly subscription of £6.99 — less than one hour of extra earnings if the tool lifts their hourly rate by even 5% — is a clear, affordable swap.

Rider costs are climbing (fuel, insurance, bike wear) while platform pay algorithms grow more opaque. At the same time, Open Banking and apps that scrape screen data make it practical for riders to share earnings in the background without manual entry. The UK’s cost‑of‑living squeeze means every quid per hour matters to a rider — a tool that reliably adds 15% to a shift’s pay gains fast word‑of‑mouth in tight‑knit rider WhatsApp groups, providing a repeatable, near‑zero‑cost growth channel.
