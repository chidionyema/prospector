Runway Rewind, undo an ex-employee's cloud access.

A tool for early-stage US software founders that finds every system a departing employee still has access to, and produces the signed offboarding record their next enterprise customer or SOC 2 auditor asks for.

A founder-operator at a 12-person startup lets someone go on a Friday. They kill the Google Workspace account and assume it is done. It is not. The person still holds a personal GitHub account with a fork, an AWS IAM key pasted into a laptop months ago, a Stripe read-only login, admin on the Slack workspace, a Vercel team seat, a Datadog invite, and a Notion guest account tied to a Gmail address. Nobody wrote any of it down, because there was never a day when writing it down was the most urgent thing.

This is shape (C): the payer is a named budget-holding role — the founder or head of engineering at a US startup under 50 people — and the recurring chore is anchored to a public framework. The tool connects, read-only, to the fifteen or so software services a startup of that size actually runs on, and answers one question per person: what does this human still touch. Then it walks the founder through cutting each one and writes a dated, per-system revocation record signed by the founder.

This borrows the way aircraft maintenance uses a sign-off card. A mechanic does not just do the work; they sign a line saying which part, when, and by whom, and that card is the airworthiness evidence, not the repair itself. The repair is easy. The card is what the regulator and the buyer actually want. Same here: revoking a GitHub seat takes eleven seconds. Producing evidence, eight months later, that it happened within a day of the person leaving is the part nobody can do.

Why it holds: the value is not the revoking, it is the accumulating record. Every month the tool runs, it builds a longer history of who had what, when it was granted, and when it was cut. That history cannot be reconstructed after the fact — most of these services either purge audit logs on the free and starter plans or keep only 90 days. Once a founder has fourteen months of clean access history sitting in the tool, moving to a rival means starting the record over at zero, on the exact artifact their enterprise deal is waiting on. That is real switching cost, and it grows every month without the operator doing anything.

The public source proving this is already paid for: the AICPA's SOC 2 trust services criteria include CC6.2 and CC6.3, which require that access is removed when it is no longer needed and that this is evidenced. NIST SP 800-53 revision 5 control AC-2(3) says the same thing in federal language. Startups selling into US enterprises get sent a security questionnaire that asks it directly, and today they answer it by hand, from memory, in a spreadsheet, the week the deal is closing.

A solo operator builds this with read-only OAuth scopes to a fixed list of services and no custom work per customer. Distribution is owned and free: publish the actual offboarding checklist for each service, free, aimed at founders searching how to remove someone from their AWS account, and let the tool be the thing that keeps the record.

Founders and heads of engineering at US software startups with 5 to 50 employees who are selling to enterprise customers — concentrated in Delaware-incorporated companies operating in California, New York and Texas — paying roughly $79 to $199 a month, usually first bought in the week a customer's security questionnaire lands.

Startup headcount at this size churns constantly, and the average startup now runs dozens of separate paid software services rather than one company network. Enterprise buyers in the United States increasingly refuse to sign without a SOC 2 report, and the access-removal criteria (CC6.2 and CC6.3 in the AICPA trust services criteria) are among the most commonly failed on a first audit because nobody kept dated evidence. Meanwhile the free and entry-level tiers of the services startups use keep audit history for only about 90 days, so the proof expires before the audit asks for it.
