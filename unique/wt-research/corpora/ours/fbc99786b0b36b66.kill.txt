PA mine-map cross-check for geothermal drilling software.

An overlay that cross-references PA's historic coal-mine maps against a proposed drill site and flags subsidence risk before the permit is filed.

PA has a deep geothermal drilling pilot zone, and PA Department of Environmental Protection (DEP) rejects a permit if the proposed bore path crosses an abandoned coal mine. PA's historic mine maps are scattered across the PA DEP Mine Map Atlas, PA Geological Survey open-file reports, and county recorder maps that exist only on paper. Geothermal drilling software vendors that want to serve PA operators need to layer all three onto a single parcel before the operator commits to the bore path.

Today, a geothermal SaaS vendor tells its PA operator customers to 'check the maps manually,' which means the operator hires a PA-licensed professional geologist for roughly $2,000 per site. The free path is the PA DEP Mine Map Atlas, which is a GIS layer that doesn't overlay cleanly onto a parcel ID and doesn't catch the unpublished paper-only maps held by county recorders.

This borrows how environmental due diligence platforms (EDR, Lightbox) overlay regulatory layers on a parcel, and applies it to PA's historic mine maps. The tool ingests the proposed drill path, geocodes it against PA parcels, and overlays three things: the PA DEP Mine Map Atlas, the PA Geological Survey's open-file reports, and a hand-curated index of the paper-only maps held by county recorders. The output is a per-bore subsidence risk report that the operator can attach to the PA DEP permit application. The drilling SaaS pays per PA project. Why this holds: the index of paper-only mine maps is owned by no one and has taken real time to compile.
