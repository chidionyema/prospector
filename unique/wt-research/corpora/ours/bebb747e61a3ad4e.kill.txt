ReleaseTrail, proves your AI app's paperwork to buyers.

A tool for UK software startups selling AI features that keeps the proof buyers and the new product safety rules ask for, ready before the deal stalls.

A founder selling an AI feature into a UK bank, an NHS trust or a council keeps hitting the same wall. The buyer's assurance team asks how the model was built, what data trained it, what it must not be used for, and who signed it off. The founder writes that from scratch every time, usually in the week the deal is meant to close.

This is a working tool, not a document store. It plugs into the startup's own GitHub repository and model registry. Each time a model or prompt is promoted to production, it captures the version, the evaluation results that were run, the datasets named in the training config, and the person who approved the merge. It then renders that history into the exact artefacts a buyer asks for: an intended-use and limitations statement, a data provenance summary, an evaluation record, and a change log per release.

This borrows the way aviation suppliers keep a part's build history so an airline can accept it years later, and applies it to model releases. In aviation nobody recreates the paperwork at the point of sale, because the record is written as the part is made. Software teams still recreate it at the point of sale, by hand, under deadline.

Why it holds: the record only exists if you were capturing it at release time. A rival arriving in month eighteen can sell the same template pack, but cannot produce the startup's own eighteen months of release history, approvals and evaluation runs. That archive is what the buyer's assurance team actually wants, and it is also what makes ripping the tool out expensive, because the audit trail lives there.

UK specifics anchor it. The Digital Regulation Cooperation Forum, which is the Competition and Markets Authority, Ofcom, the Information Commissioner's Office and the Financial Conduct Authority working together, has published guidance on auditing algorithmic systems. The ICO's own AI and data protection risk toolkit sets out exactly what a controller should record. Public sector buyers using the Crown Commercial Service G-Cloud framework and the Algorithmic Transparency Recording Standard already demand a named version of this material. The EU AI Act's technical documentation duties bite on any UK startup selling into Europe, with obligations for general-purpose model providers live from 2 August 2025.

Pricing is a flat monthly fee per startup, roughly £250 to £900 depending on how many models are tracked, plus a one-off fee to backfill the last year of releases from git history. A team of four can build and run it: the capture side is webhooks and API reads, and the rendering side is templates driven by real data.

This is the sell-to-tech shape. The buyer is the founder or the head of engineering holding the enterprise sales budget. The trigger is every enterprise or public sector security and assurance questionnaire, and every model promoted to production. The deliverable is the release-linked evidence pack.
