BodyLedger — the gig worker's injury early-warning log that reads their weekly mileage, load and pain, and tells them when to rest.

A daily app for UK delivery riders and rideshare drivers that turns weekly mileage, weight carried and pain reports into a personal MSK risk score, with stretching plans built around their shifts.

BodyLedger is a daily app for UK delivery riders and rideshare drivers that turns their mileage, weight carried, hours worked and a thirty-second pain log into a personal musculoskeletal risk score, with stretching plans built around their shift pattern.

It borrows the way physiotherapy software like Physitrack tracks a patient's MSK history over months to predict injury, and applies it to the daily physical load of the gig worker. The rider logs their day: miles cycled, kilograms carried, hours worked, pain points marked on a body diagram. After two weeks the app establishes a personal baseline. After six weeks it starts flagging trends — 'your left knee pain has been rising three days running, your load is twenty per cent above your baseline, rest tomorrow or your knee will flare.' After six months it knows your body's quirks and warns you a day or two ahead of trouble.

UK gig workers are self-employed, so they have no employer's occupational health, no statutory sick pay and no easy path to a physio. NHS Community MSK waits in England routinely exceed six weeks (NHS Digital referral-to-treatment data), and private physiotherapy is £50 or more a session — prohibitive on a tight budget. A single day off work costs a Deliveroo rider £80 to £150 in lost fees; a week off costs £500 or more. The ROI of pre-empting one injury easily covers a year's subscription. HSE records around half a million working MSK cases a year in Britain, and self-employed gig workers sit outside the employer rehab pathway — they are on their own until they cannot work.

The wedge is the proprietary data. Your body's load and pain history over months is unique to you, and the longer you use it the more accurate the predictions become — classic data compounding. Apple Health and Strava track generic fitness; they do not understand gig-specific load (a five kilogram bag carried up four flights of stairs is not the same as running five kilometres), they do not generate gig-specific risk predictions, and they do not speak the language of 'I carried eight kilo up four flights, my back hurts'. To capture this value, Apple would have to build a separate app for a sub-segment that monetises at £5 a month per user — not worth their roadmap. Strava is for cyclists who ride for sport, not for couriers who ride to carry weighted bags into tower blocks.

Acquisition is self-serve and free at the point of finding: TikTok and Instagram ads aimed at cycling communities and ride-share forums, posters at courier depots, partnerships with bike shops that see riders with MSK issues and refer them in. Retention is daily — a thirty-second log, a weekly personalised stretching plan, a monthly MSK check-in, and a push notification when risk is rising. Once the habit is set, switching means losing months of personal load history, which is exactly the friction that keeps subscribers in.

UK delivery riders and rideshare drivers aged roughly 18 to 35 who do gig work as their primary income — Deliveroo, Uber Eats, Stuart, Uber, Bolt and FreeNow workers across London, Manchester, Birmingham, Bristol and other big cities. They pay £4.99 a month or £39 a year. They are the payer because they directly feel the pain (lost income from a single week off work) and have budget authority for cheap software that pre-empts a £500-plus lost week.

Three signals stack. First, UK cycle couriers have nearly doubled since 2022 as food delivery volume grew post-pandemic — more riders means more MSK cases per month. Second, NHS Community MSK waits in England are routinely six weeks or more and private physiotherapy is £50-plus a session, so workers self-treat with ibuprofen until something breaks. Third, gig workers have no employer rehab pathway because they are self-employed under HMRC's off-payroll rules, so the cost of every avoided injury falls directly on them — which is exactly who the SaaS bills.
