Bait Sheet — the weekly rodenticide stewardship digest so a self-employed UK pest controller's next site visit is clean.

A paid weekly newsletter for self-employed UK pest controllers that decodes rodenticide stewardship rules, Wildlife Management & Licensing refusals and HSE biocide exposure incidents into a one-page weekly intel brief.

Self-employed UK pest controllers carry a BPCA or NPTA qualification, £5-£10m of public liability insurance, and a duty to follow the CRRU's rodenticide stewardship every time they set bait. A failed audit means losing client contracts, a hike in premiums, or both. The free alternative for them today is a handful of Facebook groups and a thinly circulated quarterly print press. Bait Sheet borrows how agronomists publish pest pressure bulletins — the AHDB routinely issues pest pressure alerts for cereal growers such as wheat bulb fly and aphid thresholds — and applies that same model to the working week of a professional pest controller. Every Sunday they get one page that decodes the latest CRRU resistance monitoring postcodes, Wildlife Management & Licensing Service refusal outcomes and HSE biocide exposure incidents into a 'drill these sites and pick these baits this week' brief.

Demand is rising. Defra's stewardship review tightened professional rodenticide rules from 2024 onward, more first-time applications to the Wildlife Management & Licensing Service are being refused since its digital portal launch, and HSE biocide exposure incidents under the GB Biocidal Products Regulation are being published more often. The UK pest control market was worth about £1.5bn with several thousand BPCA and NPTA member operators, most of them self-employed. Each operator visits 30-80 sites a week, and a single missed bait-resistance hot-spot is a regulatory exposure.

Distribution is free and owned. Search for 'CRRU audit check' or 'WM-LS refusal reason' surfaces almost no dedicated content today, so anchor articles on those queries capture SEO. NPTA and BPCA member forums are private to most but leak via forwards, and free excerpts posted in the operator Facebook groups drive email capture. A year-one target of 200-400 paying subscribers at £15 a month, with a free weekly excerpt tier, is plausible.

The compounding advantage is the data itself. Every weekly issue appends a structured entry — CRRU resistance postcodes, WM-LS refusal grounds, HSE incident identifiers — to a dataset no other publisher is building. A second-year paid list of even a few hundred installers is materially more useful than week one, because the underlying ledger compounds. A copycat news scrape could mimic week one, but cannot recreate the longitudinal record.

Self-employed and one-van pest controllers in the UK, typically aged 30-55, BPCA or NPTA certified, billing £45-£120 per site visit, with quarterly CPD budgets of £100-£300 and direct authority over subscription spend. They are time-poor (driving between jobs), sceptical of Facebook advice, and treat any tool that prevents a single audit failure as paying for itself in one saved contract.

HSE biocide exposure enforcement has intensified under the new GB Biocidal Products Regulation. None of this has an aggregated trade publication — the small sector still relies on a quarterly print digest and word of mouth in trade WhatsApp groups.
