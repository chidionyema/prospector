SwapDesk, sells your unused AWS reserved capacity.

A broker for finance leads at UK software firms that finds their idle AWS Savings Plans and Reserved Instances and resells the committed spend to another company, taking a cut of what is recovered.

This is a brokerage for a specific kind of stranded money inside UK software companies. A firm signs a one or three year AWS Savings Plan or Reserved Instance commitment, then migrates a workload, loses a customer, or refactors to serverless. The commitment keeps billing. AWS's own Reserved Instance Marketplace only accepts Standard Reserved Instances sold by US sellers with a US bank account, and it does not accept Savings Plans or Convertible RIs at all. So a UK company holding £40,000 of dead Savings Plan commitment has no first-party route out.\n\nThe deliverable is a transaction, not a report. The operator connects to the customer's AWS Cost Explorer via a read-only IAM role, identifies the unused commitment line by line, then matches it against a book of other UK companies whose on-demand spend fits that exact instance family and region. The transfer happens as a novation between the two companies' AWS accounts, or, where AWS will not novate, as a workload-hosting arrangement where the buyer's compute runs inside the seller's account under a written agreement and a monthly reconciliation. The broker writes the agreement, does the reconciliation, and takes 20 to 25 percent of the value transferred.\n\nThis fuses two things that are usually looked at separately: the cost-optimisation signal inside one company, and the demand signal inside another. Neither company can see the other. The broker sees both, and that two-sided view of who holds dead commitment and who is paying on-demand for the same instance family is the asset that compounds. After 100 deals the operator knows which instance families in eu-west-2 clear at what discount, and can quote a price in an hour rather than a fortnight.\n\nThe buyer role is the finance lead or Head of Engineering Ops who signed the commitment and now has to explain the variance. The recurring trigger is the monthly AWS invoice and the quarterly board pack. The public source proving the pain is already paid for is AWS's own published documentation of the Reserved Instance Marketplace eligibility rules, which exclude Savings Plans and non-US sellers outright, plus the FinOps Foundation's published framework where commitment-based discount management is a named practice with named job titles attached to it.\n\nThis is the sells-to-tech shape. The payer is a named budget-holding role inside a software company, and the chore recurs every billing cycle.
