Colour Blind Proof Deck — printed pages that show a designer's client what their brand looks like to colour-blind eyes.

A printed sample deck that shows freelance brand designers, and their clients, exactly how a colour palette fails for colour-blind readers on real paper.

Freelance designers keep losing arguments about colour on screen. A client picks a green-and-red chart palette, the designer says it will not work for colour-blind readers, and the client shrugs because on their own monitor it looks fine.

This is a physical product: a spiral-bound deck of printed cards. Each card shows one common palette pairing at full size, printed on the same coated stock a brochure uses. Facing it is the same pairing printed as it appears to someone with the three common types of colour vision deficiency. The designer puts the deck on the table in a client meeting and the argument ends in about ten seconds.

Starting from the raw problem rather than from existing tools: the pain is not that simulation software is missing — plenty exists free. The pain is that a screen simulation is easy to disbelieve and impossible to hand to someone. Paper is believed. A thing you can hold settles a disagreement that a browser tab reopens every week.

The UK grounding is specific. Public sector clients must meet the Public Sector Bodies (Websites and Mobile Applications) Accessibility Regulations 2018, which point at WCAG 2.2 AA. That standard says colour must never be the only way information is conveyed. Any designer with a council, NHS trust, university or government contract is contractually exposed to this. The deck is sold as the thing you bring to the kickoff meeting so the palette is settled before build, not after an audit.

The hard part a copyist cannot shortcut is the printing. Simulating colour vision deficiency in software is trivial; getting that simulated colour to reproduce accurately in CMYK on a specific paper stock is a colour management problem that takes real work. The operator builds and owns a correction profile for one named press and one named stock, refined over successive print runs against measured results. That profile is the asset. Anyone can copy the concept; reproducing the deck means redoing the press calibration from scratch.

One person runs this. Print runs go to a UK litho printer in batches of a few hundred. Fulfilment is a padded envelope and a Royal Mail account. Distribution is owned: the designer's own audience, plus posting spreads on the design communities where freelancers already gather. Selling price around £48 a deck, printing around £9 at a run of 500. Later versions add packs for specific sectors — charity annual reports, NHS patient leaflets, transport signage.

Freelance brand and graphic designers who work with UK public sector clients — councils, NHS trusts, universities, housing associations — where accessibility rules are written into the contract. Small design studios of two to eight people buy several decks, one per desk. Design courses at UK universities buy class sets.

The Public Sector Bodies (Websites and Mobile Applications) Accessibility Regulations 2018 apply to UK public bodies, and the Government Digital Service publishes accessibility statements naming failures. WCAG 2.2 became the recommended W3C standard in October 2023 and UK government guidance now points to it. Freelancers bidding for public work are being asked to show accessibility competence at pitch stage, and have nothing physical to show.
