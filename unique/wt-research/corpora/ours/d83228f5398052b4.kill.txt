Deletion proof reports for dating and health apps.

A tool for app founders that replays their own database backups to prove a deleted user's data really vanished, producing the evidence an enterprise buyer or the ICO asks for.

Start with the plain version. When someone asks a UK app to delete their account, the app deletes the row in the live database. The data usually survives somewhere else: nightly backups, the analytics warehouse, the customer support inbox, the recommendation cache, a partner's servers. Nobody checks. This is a piece of software the founder points at their own systems, which then hunts for that one user across every store and writes a dated report saying where the data still lives and when each copy expires.

Why anyone pays. A founder selling a dating, mental health or fertility app to a UK employer, insurer or the NHS gets a security questionnaire. One question is always some version of "describe your data deletion process and evidence of it". The founder writes a paragraph of prose. The buyer's data protection person asks for proof. The founder then spends two weeks manually grepping backups, or loses the deal. The same demand arrives from the Information Commissioner's Office when a complaint lands: under UK GDPR the right to erasure is Article 17, and the ICO can and does ask a company to demonstrate compliance, not just assert it.

The angle borrowed from elsewhere. Restaurants do not tell environmental health that the fridge is cold; they show a printed temperature log with dates. Banks do not say the trade happened; they show the reconciliation. This takes that idea and applies it to deletion. The output is not a dashboard. It is a signed PDF with a timestamp, a list of every store checked, and the exact query used, which the founder forwards to the buyer.

Why it holds. Each report teaches the tool where that company's data actually hides, and the map of hiding places gets more complete every run. After six months the founder has a deletion map nobody else has, including their own engineers, and the reports form an unbroken dated series. A buyer's auditor wants the series, not one report, so leaving means starting the evidence trail again.

What shape this is. A model does the work a data protection consultant is paid £900 a day for today, and the software runs unattended. One person can build it because the hard part is a set of connectors to Postgres, S3, BigQuery, Intercom and Segment, plus careful prompting to write the report in the register a data protection officer accepts.

The technical co-founder or head of engineering at a UK app company with 5 to 60 staff, in dating, mental health, fertility, HR or fintech, who is selling to an enterprise or public sector buyer and has been sent a security questionnaire. They typically pay £400 to £1,200 a month, and buy the moment a deal is held up.

UK GDPR keeps the right to erasure in Article 17, and the ICO publishes enforcement action for failures to honour it. Meanwhile enterprise buyers now send standard security questionnaires to even tiny suppliers, and NHS buyers require the Data Security and Protection Toolkit. Cheap object storage means startups keep years of backups they have never once searched. The gap between what the founder wrote on the questionnaire and what is actually on disk has never been wider.
