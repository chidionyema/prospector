PaperSort Visit – the mobile paperwork organiser that finds the benefits your elderly parent is missing.

A solo operator comes to your parent’s home, scans and sorts all their paperwork, flags unclaimed benefits, and leaves a tidy binder—so you stop worrying.

A 45‑year‑old NHS nurse or school business manager often has a widowed parent who lives alone in a house full of unopened envelopes. A missed hospital appointment or a reminder about pension credit sparks panic. The adult child doesn’t have the time to sort through it all, and Age UK has a 12‑week waiting list. A solo operator visits the parent’s home with a portable scanner, a shredder, and a local‑knowledge checklist. In half a day they open every drawer, scan everything into a searchable PDF on a USB stick, label and file the originals into a binder, and run the findings against a bespoke ‘hidden entitlement’ list built from previous visits. That list isn’t just standard benefits—it spells out the exact local charity grants (e.g. from the local Rotary Club or a specific church fund) and the nuances of the council’s own council tax support scheme that are never advertised. Because the operator is physically there, she also spots non‑paper evidence: a blue badge on the mantelpiece, an unclaimed attendance allowance form stuck to the fridge, a stairlift that qualifies for a VAT exemption. She leaves a ‘next steps’ sheet and an offer of a quarterly top‑up visit. The accumulated knowledge from every home—what worked where, which form won that discretionary payment—compounds into a moat no telephone‑based advice service can replicate.

A time‑pressed adult child, typically a public sector worker (nurse, teacher, council officer) in their 40s or 50s, whose widowed mother or father lives alone and has fallen behind on paperwork. They pay £150–£250 for a half‑day session, often after a crisis like a missed healthcare appointment or a threatening letter from the council. They can afford the fee because it’s a one‑off expense that might unlock thousands in unclaimed benefits.

The government’s push to make all communications digital, combined with the freeze on many pensioner benefits and the rising cost of care, means every unclaimed pound counts. Elderly people increasingly ignore paper post and cannot navigate online‑only systems, while their adult children (often squeezed public‑sector workers) are too busy to help and live too far away. This growing gap between what’s lurking in the paperwork and what’s actually claimed is a perfect opportunity for an in‑person intervention.
