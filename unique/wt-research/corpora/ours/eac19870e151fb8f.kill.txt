Locum Ledger — the fixed-fee service that gets an NHS doctor or nurse out of an overpaid agency umbrella arrangement and back into cash.

For NHS and council staff who took agency shifts through an umbrella company, we rebuild what you were actually owed and recover the deductions that were never yours to pay.

Public sector staff who pick up extra shifts through an agency are usually paid through a third company in the middle, called an umbrella company. That company takes the money from the agency, takes its own costs out, and pays what is left to the worker.

The problem is the payslip. It shows two figures the worker never agreed to: the employer's National Insurance and the Apprenticeship Levy, both taken out of the worker's own assignment rate. It also often shows a holiday pay figure that is quietly kept if it is never claimed. HMRC's own guidance on umbrella companies says the worker should be given a Key Information Document before the assignment starts, setting out exactly how the rate turns into take-home pay. In practice many are not, or the document does not match the payslips.

This is a done-for-you service, not software. The customer sends us twelve months of payslips and their assignment paperwork. We rebuild the true arithmetic shift by shift, compare it to the Key Information Document and the agency's contract, and produce a single recovery pack: the amount held back, the clause it breaches, and a demand letter. Where holiday pay was accrued and never paid, we cite the Working Time Regulations 1998 and the Employment Rights Act 1996 deductions-from-wages route to the employment tribunal. Where the deduction is unlawful, we run it as an unauthorised deduction claim. Fee is fixed, roughly £180, plus a share of anything recovered above a threshold.

This borrows a model proven somewhere completely unrelated: the freight audit firms that check a shipper's carrier invoices line by line and take a cut of the overcharges they find. Freight audit works because the buyer cannot economically check thousands of small charges themselves, but a specialist with a rebuilt rate card can. A locum's payslip is the same shape — dozens of small, opaque, individually trivial deductions that only become a real number when someone adds up a year of them.

The durable part is not the letter template. It is the rate library. Every case we run adds one more umbrella company's actual margin, actual deduction pattern, and actual response to a demand, tied to a named agency and a named NHS trust framework. After a few hundred cases we can tell a nurse on day one what her specific umbrella typically holds back and whether that firm settles or fights. No one else can assemble that, because the only people who see both the Key Information Document and the twelve months of payslips are the workers themselves, one at a time.

Growth is straightforward and repeatable. Ward-level WhatsApp groups, RCN and BMA branch reps, and the Facebook groups where locums swap agency gossip are where this spreads. One recovered £900 posted in a group of 400 nurses is the entire marketing motion. Paid acquisition works too, because the search intent is explicit and cheap.

NHS doctors, nurses and allied health professionals aged 30 to 60 who take agency or bank shifts through an umbrella company, plus supply teachers and locum social workers paid the same way. They pay a fixed fee of about £180 up front and a percentage of anything recovered. They are solvent, salaried, and the money at stake is typically £600 to £3,000 across a year.

Umbrella company use in NHS agency staffing is widespread, and from April 2026 the rules change: responsibility for getting PAYE right on umbrella-paid workers moves up the chain to the recruitment agency or the end client. That shift makes agencies and trusts far more sensitive to a worker producing evidence that an umbrella got it wrong, because the liability may now land on them. A demand letter that used to be ignored now has a recipient with real exposure.
