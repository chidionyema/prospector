SlateHaul — the school trip kit box that arrives packed, and goes back dirty.

A UK brand selling teachers a pre-packed, named-and-numbered outdoor trip kit box that ships to school before a residential and gets collected filthy afterwards, so nobody launders 30 sets of waterproofs.

This is a physical product brand, not a service desk. You sell one thing: a hard-wearing crate of school-trip kit, sized to a class, that turns up at the school office two days before the trip and is collected the day after.

The box holds 30 numbered waterproof jackets and over-trousers, 30 numbered dry bags, a first-aid kit, and a laminated head-count board. Every item carries a printed number that matches a printed register sheet. A teacher hands out number 14 and gets number 14 back. That single detail is what turns a chaotic kit handout into a two-minute job.

Two separate changes make this work now, and fusing them is the point. The first is that outdoor residential trips are being pushed back into English state schools by the Department for Education's own drive on enrichment and by Ofsted's personal development judgement, which asks what the school does beyond lessons. The second is that school budgets have no room for capital purchases of kit that sits in a cupboard for 51 weeks a year, and no staff time to wash it.

The money is straightforward. A class box rents at roughly £280 for a week, against a replacement value of about £2,400 if the school bought the same kit outright. Ten boxes in circulation, each going out 20 weeks a year, is around £56,000 of revenue on maybe £24,000 of stock. Cleaning is outsourced to a commercial launderer that already does hotel and care home contracts, so you never own a washing machine.

The durable part is not the kit — anyone can buy waterproofs. It is the numbered-item wear record you build across every return. After 200 returns you know exactly which jacket sizes get damaged, which schools return kit short, and how long a given garment survives. That record lets you price a box accurately and reject the trips that lose money, which a first-time competitor cannot do at any price.

The buyer is the teacher who runs the trip, and that matters. In most English state schools the trip lead has a delegated budget for the visit and can raise a purchase order under £500 without going to the business manager. You are selling inside that threshold on purpose.

Distribution is narrow and workable. The Outdoor Education Advisers' Panel network, the local authority outdoor education adviser in each area, and the residential centres themselves all talk to the same few hundred trip leads. You get listed by the centres as the kit option, because a centre would rather a class arrives dry than deal with 30 soaked children.

A team of three runs this: one on stock and logistics, one on school relationships, one part-time on the wear data and repairs.
