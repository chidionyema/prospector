Retention Reclaim – the sole-trader builder's fixed-fee adjudication pack that forces release of unpaid cash retentions.

A fixed-fee service that builds a statutory adjudication claim for self-employed construction workers to recover retention money held back on finished jobs.

Thousands of sole-trader builders, electricians and plasterers finish projects but wait months or years for the final slice of their pay – the retention – often a significant part of the job's profit. Main contractors routinely delay or invent defects to avoid releasing it, knowing a small operator cannot afford a solicitor or the risk of a court case. This service gives the worker a legally structured adjudication claim, assembled from a short interview and the project paperwork, that kicks open the door under the Housing Grants, Construction and Regeneration Act 1996. The Act gives a right to adjudication within 28 days of a dispute, and an adjudicator's decision is enforceable fast. The pack includes a statement of claim, a schedule of documents, and a pre-populated referral notice tailored to the specifics of the job. The worker sends it themselves and can continue to represent themselves, but the heavy legal drafting is done. This borrows the way fixed-fee flight-delay claim companies automate a statutory entitlement, applying it to the construction industry's unique retention problem. The service charges a flat £199 per claim, no win‑no fee, and only works on clear-cut cases where the paperwork is complete.

Self-employed electricians, plumbers, carpenters, bricklayers, plasterers and small builders working as subcontractors on UK construction sites.

Cash retentions in UK construction have been flagged as unfair by government reviews, but reform is slow. Small firms lost £700m in unpaid retentions in 2023 according to the Specialist Engineering Contractors’ Group. Meanwhile, adjudication is under‑used by the very people it was designed to protect: the solo operative. Public adjudication outcomes are now published, creating a dataset that reveals patterns an AI‑assisted drafter can turn into a highly specific claim with no human solicitor needed.
