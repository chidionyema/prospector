ChipYield, tells fabless startups why wafers fail.

A tool for UK fabless semiconductor startups that reads the test data coming back from a foundry and pinpoints which design blocks are killing yield.

A UK fabless chip startup sends a design to a foundry, waits three to six months, and gets back a wafer map plus a mountain of electrical test readings. Someone then has to work out why one chip in five is dead. Today that person is a contract yield engineer charging day rates, and they usually work from spreadsheets and a lot of institutional memory.

This tool does that job with a model instead. It ingests the standard test file the foundry returns (the industry calls it STDF), lines it up against the chip's own layout and block hierarchy, and says in plain terms which block, which layer and which test step the failures cluster around. The output is a short brief the founder can send back to the foundry with a specific question, rather than a general complaint.

This borrows the way aircraft engine makers read flight-recorder data to find which component is drifting before it breaks, and applies it to silicon test data that today just gets filed away.

The wedge is proprietary data that compounds. Every design a customer runs through builds a library linking failure signatures to layout patterns and to specific foundry process nodes. A foundry sees its own process across many customers but is contractually walled off from customers' design internals. A design tools vendor sees the design but never the returned test data. Only a party sitting between the two sees both, and each new customer makes the signature library better for every other one.

The UK has a real cluster to sell into: the government's National Semiconductor Strategy, published May 2023, commits up to £1bn and names design and compound semiconductors as the UK's strength, and Innovate UK funds design startups in Bristol, Cambridge and South Wales. These are venture-funded teams that can pay, and a lost tape-out costs them a funding round.
