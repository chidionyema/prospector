WageSnap – finds the wage your California public works contractor owes and builds your claim in plain English.

The tool that uncovers unpaid prevailing wages on your public construction job and generates a ready-to-file wage claim for the California Labor Commissioner.

A web tool that helps a tradesperson who worked on a California public works project figure out whether they were paid less than the legal prevailing wage, and then builds the entire claim packet they can submit themselves. It works because California requires specific wage rates (determined by the Director of Industrial Relations) for every public works project, based on craft and location. Contractors often misclassify workers or pay the wrong rate. As enforcement ramps up — more audits, more underpayments come to light — the value of a tool that helps workers prove what they are owed grows. The tool scrapes official wage determinations from the Department of Industrial Relations, matches them to the worker’s hours and craft, calculates the underpayment with liquidated damages, and produces a pre-filled DLSE complaint form and a demand letter. This borrows the approach of a tax-filing tool that guides you through a complex form, but it’s adversarial: it’s built for the worker against the employer, not for compliance. The regulator’s free complaint form cannot provide this because the state is a neutral enforcer; it cannot act as the worker’s advocate or do the calculation for them. The durable wedge is proprietary data: as more workers use the tool, it accumulates a dataset of underpayment patterns, contractor names, and project-level details that can improve success predictions and automatically flag new projects where violations are likely.

Individual manual tradespeople in California — electricians, plumbers, carpenters, laborers — who worked on a public works project (schools, roads, government buildings) and suspect they were underpaid. They pay a one-time fee of $89 to use the tool and get the completed claim packet, or $19/month for unlimited claim generation.

California’s public works enforcement is getting sharper. The Labor Commissioner’s 2022–23 annual report shows a 34% increase in prevailing wage assessments collected. Federal infrastructure money (IIJA) is flooding new projects, increasing the pool of underpaid workers. Contractors face stiffer penalties under AB 2310 (2022), which expanded personal liability for wage theft on public works. As enforcement pressure rises, the number of workers who need a fast, accurate way to claim what they’re owed will grow — but the state cannot build a tool that takes the worker’s side.
