TrialTax, the sales-tax bill your free tier is racking up.

A tool for US SaaS founders that turns free-trial and freemium usage into a live map of which states you are about to owe sales tax in, before the state finds you.

Most US states now tax software sold as a service. Since the Supreme Court decided South Dakota v. Wayfair in June 2018, a state can require you to collect its sales tax with no office and no staff there. Every state then set its own trigger: New York taxes prewritten software as a service and uses $500,000 of receipts plus 100 transactions; Pennsylvania's Sales and Use Tax Bulletin 2019-01 taxes support and canned software access at $100,000; Texas taxes 80% of a data processing charge under Rule 3.330; Washington and Chicago each have their own version.

The trap is that founders count paying customers and the states count something else. Free users still generate transactions and users in a state. A $12 monthly plan across 3,000 users in Texas crosses a threshold nobody was watching. Then a state sends a nexus questionnaire, and the founder owes back tax, interest and penalties on revenue they already spent.

This borrows the way an airline watches a flight fill up seat by seat and reprices before the plane is full. Instead of counting sales after the quarter closes, TrialTax reads your Stripe and your product analytics and shows the founder a per-state gauge that fills as signups land. It warns at 70% of a state's trigger, names the exact rule, and tells you the date registration becomes mandatory.

The buyer is the founder or the first finance hire at a seed-to-Series-B SaaS company, usually the person who signs the Stripe account. The trigger recurs every month as usage grows and every year as states change rules. The deliverable is a dated per-state exposure file with the statute cited, plus a registration-ready pack when a line goes red.

What compounds is the mapping between one product's own usage events and each state's definition of a taxable transaction. Every customer that gets a state ruling, an audit letter or a voluntary disclosure outcome feeds back a labelled example of how that state actually reads a given billing shape. After a few hundred SaaS companies, the tool knows that Texas treats a particular seat-plus-API structure one way and New York another, from observed outcomes rather than from a vendor blog. A newcomer has the statutes but not the outcome history.

This is a technology-vertical business: the ecosystem is US SaaS billing, and the rule that hurts is each state's economic nexus threshold. The public source that proves the pain is already paid for is each state revenue department's own economic nexus page and the Streamlined Sales Tax Governing Board's state guides, alongside the voluntary disclosure agreement programs states run precisely because so many software firms arrive late.

The founder or first finance hire at a US software company with $1m to $30m in annual recurring revenue and self-serve signup, typically headquartered in California, New York or Texas. They pay $400 to $2,000 a month, and more when a state letter arrives.

The Wayfair decision took effect from June 2018 and by 2024 every state with a sales tax had an economic nexus rule. States are now working through the backlog: they run nexus questionnaires and voluntary disclosure programs aimed at out-of-state software sellers. At the same time, usage-based and freemium pricing has made transaction counts explode without matching revenue.
