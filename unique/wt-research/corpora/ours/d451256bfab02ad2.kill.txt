SwitchCost — the tool that tells a small business what leaving a supplier contract actually costs, before they sign the next one.

A self-serve tool for owners of small UK businesses that reads a supplier contract and shows the real cost and date of getting out.

Someone running a five-person recruitment agency, a dental practice or a coffee roastery signs perhaps a dozen supplier contracts a year. Card payment terminals, EPOS software, waste collection, telecoms lines, water supply, coffee machine leases. Each one is signed in a hurry, in a PDF nobody reads to the end.

The money is almost never in the headline price. It is in the exit. A merchant services agreement with a 48-month minimum term. A waste contract that renews itself for another year unless you cancel in a 30-day window that opened four months before you thought about it. A telecoms line with a termination charge equal to every remaining month.

SwitchCost is a tool the owner uses themselves. They drag in the contract PDF. It comes back with four things in plain words: the date the contract actually ends, the last day you can give notice without it rolling over, what it costs to leave today, and what it costs to leave on each of the next twelve months. It puts those notice dates in the owner's calendar as reminders. When a notice date arrives, it generates the cancellation letter with the right clause reference and the right address.

This starts from the raw pain rather than from how the market solves it today. The market's answer is a broker who switches you and takes a commission from the new supplier. That broker has no reason to tell you your best move is to stay put for eight more months. The owner's real question is not 'who is cheapest' — it is 'when am I free, and what does freedom cost'. Nobody sells the answer to that question, because the answer is often 'do nothing yet'.

The growth motion is the contract itself. Every business that signs a contract has a counterparty, an accountant and a bookkeeper. The free version reads one contract and gives the exit date. The paid version, around £29 a month, watches the whole portfolio, sends the notice reminders and writes the letters. Accountants and bookkeepers become the volume channel: a bookkeeper serving 40 small businesses can run every client's supplier contracts through it, which is exactly the sort of extra value they can charge for.

What compounds is the clause library. Every contract uploaded teaches the tool how a specific supplier's paperwork works. After a few thousand uploads, it knows that a named waste firm's 2023 template uses a 90-day notice window and its 2025 template uses 60, that a particular card processor's early termination formula is stated in a schedule rather than the body, and which suppliers routinely apply charges their own contract does not support. That is a body of knowledge about real supplier paperwork that only accumulates by seeing thousands of signed copies. No supplier will publish it, and no comparison site sees it, because comparison sites see prices and not signed contracts.

UK grounding sits in the enforcement layer. Small businesses have been able to complain to the Financial Ombudsman Service since April 2019 if they have fewer than 50 staff and turnover under £6.5m, which covers merchant services and business banking disputes. Ofcom's rules on business telecoms contracts and the Competition and Markets Authority's work on auto-renewing contracts give the tool real citations to put in a cancellation letter. Energy contracts for microbusinesses carry Ofgem rules on renewal notice periods and broker commission disclosure that came into force in December 2022. The letter the tool generates cites the specific rule that applies to that supplier type, which is why suppliers respond to it.
