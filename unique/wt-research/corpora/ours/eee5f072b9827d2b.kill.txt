Grave Space Rescue — the fixed-fee service that reopens a family's right to a burial plot the council says lapsed.

For a fixed £340 fee, we rebuild the paperwork that proves a family still owns a burial plot, so a council or cemetery cannot resell it or refuse a second burial.

When someone dies, families often discover the plot they thought they owned cannot be used. The council says the paper deed is lost, the burial rights ran out, or the records do not match the name on the grave. The family is grieving, has a funeral booked, and has days to fix it.

We sell one thing: a finished evidence pack that gets the burial rights reinstated or transferred, delivered inside seven working days.

In England and Wales a burial plot is not land you own. You buy an Exclusive Right of Burial for a fixed term, usually 50, 75 or 99 years, under the Local Authorities' Cemeteries Order 1977. The right can be renewed. It can also be transferred to a family member after the original holder dies, but only with the right paperwork. Most councils will accept a statutory declaration plus proof of the chain of relationship when the original deed is lost. Very few families know this, and funeral directors are not paid to do it.

Our work is unglamorous and repeatable. We pull the burial register entry from the cemetery office, order the death and marriage certificates from the General Register Office that link the original grantee to the person asking, check whether the term has expired and what the council's own charging schedule says the renewal costs, draft the statutory declaration for a solicitor or commissioner for oaths to witness, and submit the transfer or renewal application on the family's headed form. Where the council has already served notice to reclaim the plot, we respond inside the notice period.

This widens out from one narrow moment to the whole life of the plot. We also sell a £95 check for people who are not yet bereaved: tell us the cemetery and the grave, and we tell you who legally holds the right, when it expires, and what it costs to extend. That is the version that spreads by word of mouth in family WhatsApp groups.

The durable part is the file we build. Every council runs its cemeteries differently: different renewal terms, different fees, different evidence they will accept for a lost deed, different appetite for reclaiming old graves. After a few hundred jobs we know, per cemetery, which named officer decides, what wording they accept in a declaration, and how long they take. That knowledge is not published anywhere and cannot be scraped, because it comes from doing the job. A rival starting today has to buy it one refused application at a time.

A team of three runs this. One person does research and drafting, one handles the phone calls to cemetery offices, one sells. Volume comes from funeral directors, monumental masons and probate solicitors who hit this problem constantly and have no way to solve it.

The person aged 30 to 60 arranging a parent's funeral, very often an NHS nurse, teacher, council officer or civil servant who is the organised one in the family. They pay because the alternative is a new plot, which typically costs £1,500 to £4,500 in a council cemetery and far more in London, and because burying a parent away from a spouse or grandparent is not something they will accept. Referrals come from independent funeral directors and monumental masons, who lose sales when a memorial cannot be added to a grave whose rights have lapsed.

Cemeteries in older urban areas are running out of space, so councils are actively reclaiming plots whose burial rights have expired. The Local Authorities' Cemeteries Order 1977 already lets them do this, and rising pressure on burial land plus squeezed council budgets means more of them are now doing it in practice, serving notices on graves that have sat undisturbed for decades. At the same time cremation costs have risen and burial fees with them, so the value of an old family plot has gone up sharply just as the paperwork proving ownership has decayed.
