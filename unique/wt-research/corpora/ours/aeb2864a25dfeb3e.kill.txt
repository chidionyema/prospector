Session Fee Rescue — the tool that gets a session musician paid when a track they played on gets released.

For freelance session players and singers in the UK, this tool tracks which recordings you performed on and forces the release payments and royalties you are legally owed.

A session musician plays on a track for a day rate. Months later the track appears on a streaming service, in an advert, or on a TV show. The player often never hears about it and never gets the extra money that release triggers.

This is a tool the player uses themselves. You log every session as you do it: the date, the studio, the artist, the producer who booked you, the working title, and what you played. You photograph the session sheet on your phone. The tool then watches for that recording surfacing in the world.

It does this by matching your logged sessions against public release data. It reads new release feeds from streaming catalogues, PPL's public repertoire search, and the credits listed on labels' own releases. When a working title or an artist plus a date window lines up with one of your logged sessions, it flags it.

Then it does the paperwork. UK session work under the Musicians' Union agreements carries specific extra payments when a recording is used beyond the original purpose. There are set fees for use in adverts, for film and TV, and for release. Separately, PPL pays performers a share whenever a recording is played on radio, in shops, or on TV. To get that share you must be registered on the recording at PPL, and the person who registers it is usually the label, not you.

The tool generates the PPL performer registration claim with your evidence attached, and generates the letter to the booking producer or label citing the specific Musicians' Union agreement clause and the fee due. The player sends it. Most get paid because the paperwork is correct and the claim is small enough to settle rather than argue.

Why this holds up over time. Working from the raw problem rather than from how the industry does it today: the fundamental fact is that the performer is the only person present at the session who has an interest in recording what happened, and nobody currently records it in a machine-readable way. The session sheet is a piece of paper that goes in a drawer. So the tool accumulates something nobody else has — a timestamped, performer-verified log of who played on what, growing session by session across thousands of players.

That log becomes the thing rivals cannot get. After two years the tool knows the actual personnel on recordings that have no published credits at all, because five different players independently logged the same date at the same studio and the entries corroborate each other. A label cannot build this — it only knows its own sessions. PPL cannot build it — it only receives what labels choose to file. The data is created by the act of using the tool, which means a copycat starting later starts at zero and stays behind.

The player pays a low monthly amount to keep logging, and the tool takes a share of anything recovered above a threshold. Growth comes from the corroboration itself: when your logged session matches another player's entry, the tool tells you a colleague on that date is already registered, and asks whether to invite the rest of the band. Session work is done by small, repeating groups of people, so each recovered claim naturally pulls in the four other people who were in the room.

Freelance session musicians, backing vocalists and orchestral players in the UK who do paid studio and live-to-broadcast work — the roughly 30,000 people who hold Musicians' Union membership and take booked session work. They pay a small monthly fee plus a share of money recovered. Secondary payers are music production companies who want clean personnel records for their own back catalogue.

Streaming has made it normal for a recording to appear publicly within weeks of a session, so the gap between playing and release is short enough that a player can still remember and prove what they did. At the same time PPL now distributes hundreds of millions of pounds a year to performers, and its performer registration route is open to individuals rather than only labels. Recent industry attention on how little session players earn from streaming, including the government's own inquiry into music streaming economics, has made producers and labels far more reluctant to be seen refusing a documented session claim.
