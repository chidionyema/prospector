PensionPass — the workbook that decides what your children inherit from your pension, and what HMRC keeps.

A self-fill workbook for UK retirees with a defined contribution pension that maps what HMRC will inherit from the pot in April 2027, then walks them through the legal ways to shrink that bill.

The product is a self-fill workbook — a 40-page PDF with linked spreadsheets — that walks a UK retiree with a defined contribution pension through exactly what HMRC will treat as part of their estate when they die after April 2027, then through the legal ways to shrink that bill.

It works because the policy is new, the deadline is short, and most affected people have never heard of it. From 6 April 2027, the Finance Act 2024 brings unused defined contribution pension funds inside the Inheritance Tax net for the first time — a 40% tax on anything over £325,000 (plus the £175,000 residence band if you leave the home to direct descendants), with the thresholds frozen until April 2030. A retiree with a £300,000 pension and a £400,000 home will be taxed on the pension for the first time. Most affected people are aged 60-75 — the same cohort auto-enrolled into workplace pensions from 2012.

This is built around a rule HMRC is making more aggressive each year — frozen thresholds and the new inclusion of pensions — so the value of the workbook compounds with enforcement intensity. HMRC's own guidance and the new MoneyHelper explainer cover the policy in the abstract, but HMRC structurally cannot advise a taxpayer how to shrink their own bill, and a regulated financial adviser charges £2,000-£5,000 for the personal advice that gates most retirees out. The workbook sits between these — it gives the buyer the personalised calculations, the decision flowcharts, and the exact questions to put to a free initial meeting with an adviser, so the buyer arrives with the right plan and pays only for execution.

It holds because the rule is large and time-bounded: every affected retiree needs to act before April 2027, and the workbook is the cheapest credible route to doing that without paying an adviser. Distribution runs through Facebook groups and personal-finance newsletters read by 60-75 year-olds and their adult children — the cohort with both budget authority and motivation. The workbook updates each year as HMRC publishes guidance, so buyers re-buy on each annual edition, and the durable core is the structured thinking rather than the rule itself — even after April 2027, every retiree with a DC pension still needs the same workbook to model their position, and it remains the only consumer product that explicitly walks them through the legal avoidance menu.

Over-50s Facebook groups, retirement-focused Substack newsletters, and age-friendly money podcasts are populated and reachable for a solo operator with paid ads or guest contributions.
