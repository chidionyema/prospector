The Bench-Weld Round — The NHS Estates & Facilities Technician's Rehab-Equipment Structural Recertification Round.

A mobile bench that visits NHS community rehab stores and independent hospices on a fixed 90-day round to load-prove, weld-repair and stamp-certify walking frames, hoisting slings, transfer boards and rise-recliners that are currently binned at the first sign of a cracked weld — charging per certified item returned to circulation, not per repair attempted.

CROSS_SECTOR LENS: transplant the aviation MRO 'component overhaul with life-extension certificate' model — where a scrapped-value part is returned to service with a signed release-to-service and an accrued cycle history — into NHS community equipment stores, which currently run a binary condemn/replace decision because no one owns a repeatable structural proof method. A Band 5-7 estates technician or community equipment store manager physically watches ~£40-90k a year of aluminium walking frames, ceiling-hoist slings and steel transfer aids get condemned on visual inspection alone, because the store's only options are (a) trust it and carry personal liability if it fails under a patient, or (b) scrap it. The friction is daily and personal: they sign the condemn sheet. The physical_ops form is native here — the value is created by a person with a load rig, a TIG welder, a dye-penetrant kit and a stamping die physically standing in the store on a scheduled round, not by software. Monetisation is per item stamped and returned to the store's asset register at a price set as a fixed fraction of replacement cost, so the buyer's saving is arithmetic and visible on the same page as the invoice. The moat is proprietary_data: every item that passes the bench generates a load-curve, an alloy/weld-geometry record and a failure-mode entry tied to a specific manufacturer, model and production year — after two years of rounds the operator holds the only failure-population dataset on post-service-life UK community rehab equipment, which lets them certify items competitors must condemn, and lets them price the round by predicted pass rate per store. Manufacturers structurally cannot build this dataset (they see returns, not the surviving population) and equipment stores cannot (each holds too few items of any one model). One person runs it: a van, a two-day-a-week round of 4-6 stores within a 60-mile radius, batch bench work the other days, and AI-drafted certification packs from photographed rig output.

NHS community equipment store managers and Band 6-7 estates/rehab-equipment technicians holding a devolved consumables and replacement budget (typically £60-250k/yr) inside integrated community equipment services, plus independent hospice and care-home group facilities leads in the same catchment — the named individual signs off the round because the invoice is provably below the replacement spend they would otherwise book, and it is their name on the condemn sheet either way.

Community equipment replacement budgets have been squeezed hard against rising aluminium and steel input costs while post-pandemic hospital-discharge-to-home volumes have permanently raised the throughput of loan equipment through community stores, so the condemn-and-replace loop now consumes a visibly larger share of a flat budget. Simultaneously, cheap portable load-test rigs, handheld hardness testers and consumer-grade dye-penetrant kits have fallen to a price a single operator can own outright, and NHS estates teams have lost in-house workshop capacity through years of headcount attrition — the physical skill left the building at exactly the moment the arithmetic started demanding it back.
