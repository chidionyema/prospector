Kit Deposit, tools for teachers who buy their own.

A fixed-fee service that gets school staff their money back for classroom supplies and equipment they paid for out of their own pocket, using the school's own spending rules.

Teachers and teaching assistants routinely buy books, art materials, science kit and prizes with their own money. A 2024 Sutton Trust survey of over 10,000 UK teachers found large numbers spending personal money on classroom resources and even food for pupils. Almost none of it is reclaimed, because the reimbursement route inside a school is buried in a finance policy nobody reads and gatekept by a business manager the teacher does not want to annoy.

Kit Deposit is a paid service, not an app. A member of staff sends photos of receipts and the name of their school. The service pulls that school's or trust's published finance policy — academy trusts must publish a scheme of delegation and their spending rules, and maintained schools operate under the local authority's scheme for financing schools — and produces one clean claim: the exact policy clause that authorises the spend, the correct form, the right approver, and a covering note written in the tone of a routine administrative request rather than a complaint. Price is a flat £39 per claim bundle, or £15 a term for staffroom groups of ten or more.

This borrows the way medical billing companies work in the United States. Doctors there do not chase insurers themselves; a specialist firm codes the claim to the payer's own rulebook and takes a fee, because the payer's rules are knowable but tedious. The same thing is true of English school finance: every academy trust's rules are public, they differ from each other, and no individual teacher will ever learn more than one of them.

The asset that compounds is a library of what each named trust and local authority actually approves, and what it refuses. After a few hundred claims the service knows that one trust reimburses display materials but not food, that another needs pre-approval above £25, and that a third pays within 14 days. That mapping cannot be bought or scraped from policy documents alone, because it is built from real outcomes. It gets more valuable as multi-academy trusts grow, and it makes each new claim faster to produce than the last.

Growth runs through staffroom word of mouth and the education unions' local branches, then through a per-trust motion: once twenty staff in one trust have claimed, the service knows that trust cold and can sell into every school in it.

Classroom teachers, teaching assistants and SENCOs in English state schools, especially those in large multi-academy trusts, paying a flat fee per claim. Secondary buyer: staffroom groups and union branch reps buying a termly group rate for their members.

Real-terms pay for school staff has fallen while school budgets have tightened, so out-of-pocket spending is both more common and more painful. The shift of most secondary schools into multi-academy trusts means finance rules are now set trust by trust and published, which makes them mappable for the first time.
