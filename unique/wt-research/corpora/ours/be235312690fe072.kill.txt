Provenance audits for AI labs in copyright suits.

A fixed-fee pack that turns an AI lab's training data inventory into a court-ready chain-of-custody declaration, built for AI companies named in NYT v. OpenAI-style litigation.

A small California firm runs provenance audits for AI labs caught up in the current wave of US copyright lawsuits. The audit takes the lab's training data inventory (web crawls, licensed corpora, books, songs, images), verifies each source's licence, runs an opt-out verification against known rights-holder lists, and signs a chain-of-custody declaration the lab's lawyers hand to opposing counsel and the court.

Why it works — there is a real docket. NYT v. OpenAI (S.D.N.Y. 1:23-cv-11155, filed December 2023), Authors Guild v. OpenAI (1:23-cv-08292), Bartz v. Anthropic (N.D. Cal. 3:24-cv-05417, filed August 2024), UMG v. Suno (D. Mass. 1:24-cv-11611) and RIAA v. Udio (1:24-cv-11593), both filed June 2024 — together put the training data of every US-domiciled generative AI lab in scope. Discovery demands already ask labs to produce every URL, every book, every song they ingested. Mid-sized labs (Suno, Udio, Midjourney, Stability AI, Runway) do not have 50-lawyer teams; they spend one to three million dollars per suit just gathering what their crawlers already kept, because the data lives in engineering logs and not in a structured record a court will accept. The US Copyright Office's Compendium of U.S. Copyright Office Practices, Third Edition (December 2024) sets out how courts weigh fair-use evidence, which is the standard the declaration must address.

Why it holds — this borrows the Drug Master File format the FDA uses for pharmaceutical ingredient provenance. Each new training dataset the lab ingests becomes a numbered, time-stamped record with source, licence, and opt-out evidence. The proprietary edge is the cross-lab, cross-suit record: we have seen what survives motions to compel, what gets challenged, and what gets accepted. A single lab cannot build that knowledge; a plaintiff-side firm only sees its own client; a defence-side firm works one engagement at a time. The US Copyright Office and the courts cannot take a commercial side, cannot run discovery for a private party, and cannot serve the regulated party adversarially. Our deliverable sharpens with every dataset we log, which is the compounding pattern a regulator's free tool structurally cannot replicate.
