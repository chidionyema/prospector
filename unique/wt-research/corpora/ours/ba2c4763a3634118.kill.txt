Repro Rig — the parts kit that turns one old phone or board into a testing device you can hand to a client.

A boxed kit of cables, mounts, taps and a pre-flashed control stick that turns junk-drawer phones and single-board computers into a tidy, always-on test bench for freelance developers.

This is a physical product business, sold direct from one website, aimed at freelance and contract software developers in the UK who build mobile apps, embedded firmware, or hardware-adjacent web work.

The kit is a box. Inside: a powered USB hub with per-port switching, right-angle cables cut to length, a laser-cut rack that holds four phones or four small boards upright, a relay board that can power-cycle each slot, and a pre-flashed control stick that exposes each device over the network. There is a printed setup card with six steps. No soldering.

The pain is small and daily. A developer testing an app across Android versions ends up with three phones face-down on the desk, a nest of cables, and one that keeps dropping off the USB bus. A developer working on a Raspberry Pi product has to walk over and pull the plug every time the board hangs. Both problems are boring, both cost an hour a week, and neither is worth building a rig for from scratch.

Two separate changes make this sellable now, and fusing them is the point. First, phones have become disposable in a specific new way: since 26 December 2023 the UK has required almost all new phones sold here to charge over USB-C, under the rules implementing the EU common charger directive that the UK mirrored for Northern Ireland and that manufacturers then applied across their whole UK range. That collapsed the cable problem — a kit no longer needs six connector variants. Second, cheap phones now get long official support: Google commits to seven years of Android updates on Pixel 8 and later, and Samsung offers seven years on its recent Galaxy S range. So a five-year-old handset bought secondhand is still a legitimate test target, not landfill. Together those two facts mean a developer's drawer of old phones is suddenly worth racking, and one cable type will do it.

Money works like a normal product business. A core kit sells at £180 to £240. Cost of goods, assembled in the UK in small runs, sits near £70 to £90 — hub, relay board, laser-cut acrylic or ply, cables, the control stick, box and print. That is a gross margin around 60 percent, before postage. Add-ons carry the repeat revenue: extra four-slot racks at £45, replacement cable sets at £18, a mains-side switched strip at £55, and a version with a small camera arm at £120 for people testing screens and LED indicators. Average order value climbs when someone racks a second bench.

The durable part is the accumulated fit knowledge. Every phone model, dev board and single-board computer has a different thickness, port position, button placement and charging quirk. The business ships a rack, gets photos and complaints back, and cuts a better rack. After two hundred orders it owns a fitment table — which exact handsets and boards sit properly in which slot, at which cable length, with which power quirk — that nobody can copy without shipping the same two hundred boxes and eating the same returns. That table drives the product page ('will it fit my Pixel 6a? yes, slot B, 0.3m cable'), which is also the thing that converts browsers into buyers. A generic hub seller has no reason to build it and no returns data to build it from.

Distribution is honest and small-operator sized. Developers who want this are findable: r/androiddev, embedded and Rust hardware forums, Hackaday, the Raspberry Pi community, and conference sponsor tables at events like EMF Camp and Bristol's hardware meetups. Paid acquisition works because the search intent is specific and cheap — people already search for USB hub per-port power switching and phone test rack. One founder plus one part-time assembler can pack and post 300 boxes a month from a lock-up.

Legal side is clean. This is a mains-adjacent accessory kit, so it needs UKCA marking, a declaration of conformity, electrical safety and electromagnetic compatibility testing on the powered parts, and compliance with the UK's electrical equipment safety rules. Buying the hub and power supply as already-certified components and treating the rack as a passive fixture keeps the testing bill modest. Registering under the WEEE rules for electrical products is a small annual cost.

Freelance and contract software developers in the UK, mostly one-person limited companies, who build Android apps, embedded firmware, or products with a Raspberry Pi or similar board inside. They expense it through the company at around £200, which is a normal tool purchase for someone billing £400 to £600 a day. Small agencies with two to eight developers buy several and become the repeat buyers for extra racks.

Since 26 December 2023 nearly every new phone sold in the UK charges over USB-C, so a testing kit only needs one cable type instead of six. At the same time Google and Samsung both now promise seven years of software updates on their recent phones, which means cheap secondhand handsets stay useful as test targets for years. Developers therefore have a growing pile of usable old phones and one standard cable, and nothing tidy to put them in.
