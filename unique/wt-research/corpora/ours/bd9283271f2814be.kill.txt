TribunalTrace — the evidence bundle that turns a council's SEND refusal into a tribunal win.

A tool that builds a parent's appeal bundle against a council's refusal or watered-down Education, Health and Care Plan, matched against past tribunal decisions against that same council.

When a council in England turns down a request for an Education, Health and Care Plan (EHCP) for a child with special educational needs, or agrees a plan that gives far less support than the child needs, the parent's only real route is the First-tier Tribunal (Special Educational Needs and Disability), set up under the Children and Families Act 2014. Most parents never appeal, not because their case is weak but because building the evidence bundle is exhausting: educational psychologist reports, speech and language assessments, the council's own case notes, and case law that shows a tribunal panel what 'reasonable provision' has meant in similar cases. Councils know this. A parent who does go all the way to a hearing wins at least part of their case in the great majority of decisions, a pattern the Department for Education's own tribunal statistics have shown for years. The gap is not the law, it is the paperwork.

TribunalTrace is a self-serve tool built for that gap. A parent enters their child's diagnosis, the council's refusal letter, and the professional reports they already have. The tool drafts the structured appeal bundle, flags the missing evidence a panel usually asks for, and cites the closest-matching past decisions against that specific council — pulled from a growing archive built by filing Freedom of Information requests to every local authority for their SEND appeal and settlement records, since the tribunal service does not publish a searchable public database of full decisions the way other tribunals do (child privacy is the usual reason given). No charity or government body currently logs, per council, how often that authority settles before a hearing, what it argues, and what it loses on — because doing that at scale means treating 150+ local authorities as an adversary, one FOI request at a time, and organising the results into a product. That is not a job a council, the tribunal service, or a stretched charity has any reason to take on.

The value compounds with every council's own behaviour: the accounting rule that has let English councils keep special needs overspends off their main books, rather than count against their reserves, was due to end this year, so every council now has a sharper financial reason to refuse or minimise new plans. As refusals rise, the archive of decisions against each council grows, and the tool's precedent matching gets more precise for the next parent facing the same authority.

Parents aged 35 to 55 in professional jobs, with a mortgage and other children to manage, who are fighting their local council over their child's EHCP. They already have the professional reports and the refusal letter; they are short on time and confidence, not money, and will pay a fixed fee (around £295 per appeal, or a lower monthly fee while the case is open) rather than pay a solicitor several thousand pounds or lose the appeal by default because the bundle never got built.

SEND appeal numbers have risen sharply in England as council high-needs budgets have come under sustained strain, and the temporary accounting rule that let councils carry special needs deficits off their core books without it counting against their reserves was due to lapse this year, giving every authority a harder financial incentive to refuse or reduce plans. That directly increases the number of parents who need to appeal and the stakes of each refusal.
