LatePayWatch — The weekly bulletin exposing construction firms that sit on invoices.

A paid weekly newsletter that names and shames the UK's worst construction late payers, built on crowd-sourced data from tradespeople.

This borrows the way credit rating agencies profile companies, but instead uses direct reports from electricians, plumbers and builders who have been left unpaid. A solo journalist compiles reports, public payment-practice data, and tribunal rulings into a simple digest with red-flag alerts and payment timelines. The channel lives on a Substack-style model, charging £9/month for early access and a searchable archive. Over time, the archive compounds into a unique risk database no single tradesperson could build alone—making it an essential pre-quote check for anyone in the trades. The cross-sector insight is that the same methodology financial analysts use to rate corporate creditworthiness can be packaged as a trade-focused, plain-English newsletter that names names, rather than a faceless score. Because it relies on human-sourced tips, it delivers colour a government dataset never will, such as exactly which quantity surveyor habitually 'loses' paperwork.

Self-employed electricians, plumbers, carpenters, roofers, and general builders who quote work to main contractors, small developers, and commercial clients. They pay to avoid spending weeks chasing invoices or writing off bad debt.

Late payment in UK construction is endemic and worsening as material costs rise and cashflow tightens. The Reporting on Payment Practices and Performance Regulations 2017 force large firms to publish data, but it is trapped in PDFs no sole trader reads. Meanwhile, the Small Business Commissioner lacks teeth. A dedicated, adversarial newsletter fills the void by interpreting the data, adding crowd-sourced anecdotes, and delivering a simple risk checklist right when a tradesperson is deciding whether to take a job. The moment is right because the cost-of-living crisis makes every unpaid invoice an existential threat, increasing willingness to pay for protection.

A free official UK service already lets anyone check real company payment track records from government data, which shows people are expected to look up who pays bills and when.
