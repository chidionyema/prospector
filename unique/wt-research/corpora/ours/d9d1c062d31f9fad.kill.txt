Deprecated cloud SKU alerts for public sector IT buyers.

A weekly intelligence brief telling state and city IT operations managers which cloud and software SKUs on their signed contract vehicles have been quietly discontinued, before renewal locks them in.

An operations manager at a US state agency or a mid-sized city IT department buys nearly all cloud and software through a small number of contract vehicles: NASPO ValuePoint, the state's own term schedules, GSA Multiple Award Schedule 70 lines, and cooperative agreements like Sourcewell. The line items on those vehicles are frozen catalogues. The vendors, meanwhile, retire products continuously.

The daily friction is this. The operations manager renews a line item because it is on the approved vehicle and procurement will not fight them on it. Nine months later the vendor's end-of-life notice lands, the product stops receiving security patches, and the agency is running an unsupported service it just paid a year for. Nobody inside the agency is watching the gap between what the contract catalogue still sells and what the vendor still supports.

The business is a paid weekly brief plus a searchable back catalogue. Each week it reconciles two things nobody reconciles today: the live SKU lists published on GSA Advantage and on state schedule portals, against vendor end-of-sale and end-of-support announcements scraped from AWS, Microsoft, Google Cloud, Cisco, VMware/Broadcom, Oracle and Salesforce lifecycle pages. Where a SKU is still orderable but the underlying product is past end-of-sale, that is a named finding, with the vehicle number, the line item, the vendor's own notice date, and the renewal window it threatens.

This turns the usual direction of the work around. A regulator or a procurement office can publish rules and can publish its own catalogue. It structurally cannot publish a running list of the ways its own catalogue is stale, because that list is an accusation against its own vendor management. It also cannot take the agency buyer's side against a vendor it holds a master agreement with. The value here grows precisely as procurement oversight gets more aggressive, and the body imposing the duty is the one body that will never ship it.

The compounding asset is the reconciliation history. After a year the brief knows which vendors habitually leave dead SKUs on public schedules, how many months of lag each one runs, and which state vehicles are slowest to purge. That lag table cannot be bought and cannot be reconstructed retroactively, because vendor lifecycle pages are overwritten rather than archived. It is built only by watching, weekly, from a fixed start date.

This is a sells-to-tech play in reverse: the payer is a budget-holding operations manager inside government IT, the recurring trigger is the renewal calendar of a named contract vehicle, and the deliverable is a dated finding they forward to procurement. Public proof the pain is paid for: GSA publishes SIN and SKU-level catalogue data on GSA Advantage and eBuy, and agency inspectors general repeatedly report unsupported software findings — the federal CIO's own FISMA metrics require agencies to report unsupported software counts, which means somebody is already staffed to chase this.

A team of two to six runs it. One person owns the scraping and reconciliation, one writes the weekly findings in plain language, one sells. Pricing is a per-seat annual subscription in the low thousands of dollars, sold to named IT operations managers in states and large counties, with the first year sold off a free sample brief naming three real dead SKUs on that reader's own state vehicle.
