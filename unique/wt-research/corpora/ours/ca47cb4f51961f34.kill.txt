PackDown — the fixed-fee sort-and-sell round for the bedroom of a child who has moved into supported living.

For a fixed £480, one person spends two days emptying, sorting and selling the contents of a disabled young adult's old room, and hands the carer a cash total plus a written list of what went where.

This is a two-day job you buy once. A family carer's disabled son or daughter turns 18 to 25 and moves into supported living or a residential placement. The bedroom left behind is full of outgrown specialist things: a Rifton chair, a bath seat, sensory tent, weighted blankets, a mountain of adapted toys, plus five years of paperwork from the local authority. The carer cannot face it. They also cannot just skip it, because some of that kit belongs to the council or the NHS wheelchair service, some is worth real money second-hand, and some of the paperwork must be kept.

The service is simple and physical. You arrive, sort the room into four piles, and each pile has a defined destination. Pile one is council or NHS loan equipment, which goes back to the community equipment store with a collection reference so the family stops being liable for it. Pile two sells: adapted trikes, standing frames, sensory equipment and postural seating hold their value, and there are active buyer pools on Facebook groups and specialist forums. Pile three is donated to a local family through a parent-carer forum, which is the emotionally important pile. Pile four is disposed of. You photograph, list, ship and hand over the money, minus nothing — the fee is the fee.

Why this holds for one person without capital: you are not buying stock, not holding inventory, and not running a shop. You take a fixed fee for labour and judgment. The judgment is the part that compounds. After thirty rooms you know that a Leckey Squiggles seat sells in four days at around £300 while a hoist sling sells for almost nothing, you know which councils actually collect loaned equipment and which need three phone calls, and you know which local charities take what. That pricing knowledge lives in your notebook, not on any public site, because completed sale prices on private Facebook groups and specialist forums are not published anywhere searchable.

The legality is clean, and being clear about it is part of the sell. You do not sell anything that is a medical device requiring reissue checks — hoists, slings and anything load-bearing goes back or goes to recycling, never onward. You sell furniture, seating, sensory items and mobility aids that carry no reconditioning duty. You are not giving advice on benefits or on the placement.

Distribution is free and specific. Every English local authority must run a Parent Carer Forum, and there are around 150 of them, each with a mailing list and a Facebook group. Transition to adulthood is the single most discussed topic on those forums, because the Children and Families Act 2014 requires local authorities to plan it from Year 9. You post one honest thread about what you found in the first ten rooms and what it sold for. That is the whole marketing plan.

Pricing: £480 for a standard bedroom within 25 miles, £680 for a whole flat or two rooms, plus a 20% share of anything selling above £400 so a lucky find pays you both. One person can do two rooms a week alongside the listing work, which is about £4,000 a month at full load.

Parents and family carers of a disabled young person aged 16 to 25 who has just moved into supported living, residential care or their own adapted flat — typically in the six weeks after the move, when the room is a daily reminder and the council is asking about its loaned equipment. Also paid by adult siblings who have taken over caring duties and inherited a house full of someone else's kit.

The move from children's to adult social care is being pushed through faster and in bigger numbers. NHS England's Building the Right Support programme has been moving people with learning disabilities and autism out of hospital and into community settings, and local authorities are under pressure to complete transition planning under the Children and Families Act 2014. At the same time, community equipment stores across England are actively chasing families to return loaned kit because replacement budgets are squeezed, so an uncollected hoist is now a letter rather than a shrug. Second-hand demand is the other half: specialist paediatric seating and adapted trikes cost £1,500 to £4,000 new, families are waiting months for statutory provision, and the private resale groups for this equipment have grown into the tens of thousands of members.
