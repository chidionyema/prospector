SlipRisk Bond — The Sole-Trader Resin-Bound Driveway Installer's Post-Cure Slip-Test Failure Income Micro-Bond.

A subscription micro-bond that pays a named resin-bound surfacing installer a fixed daily income while a customer-commissioned pendulum slip test (BS 7976 / UKSRG) has failed and the driveway is disputed, in exchange for exclusive rights to the installer's per-batch cure telemetry.

Lens: narrow. Resin-bound gravel is one of the fastest-growing UK domestic surfacing trades and is dominated by genuinely solo installers — one van, one mixer, aggregate and UV-stable resin bought per job. The specific daily friction is not compliance: it is that resin-bound is laid at 6mm over a mixture of ambient temperature, humidity and substrate moisture that the installer cannot fully control, and when a homeowner slips six months later the entire dispute turns on a pendulum test value (PTV) commissioned by the homeowner or their insurer. A failed PTV puts the installer into an unpaid remediation stand-off lasting weeks — the drive must be re-topped or fully lifted, the homeowner withholds the balance, and there is no insurance product that pays anything during the argument because public liability responds only to injury claims, not to a workmanship dispute over a surface's measured slip value. The structural form is risk_financing, built natively: the installer pays a small monthly premium and, on the objective trigger of a third-party PTV report below the 36 threshold on their installation, receives a fixed daily sum for up to 45 days while the dispute runs. It is not insurance-as-a-wrapper because the whole economic point is the underwriting asset: to hold the bond the installer must submit, per pour, a photo of the batch, the resin-to-aggregate ratio, the ambient temp/RH reading and the substrate moisture reading through a phone flow that takes 90 seconds. That builds a compounding proprietary dataset linking cure conditions to eventual PTV outcomes at the batch level — data no insurer, no resin manufacturer and no trade body can obtain, because none of them are paying the installer for the discipline of recording it. Within two seasons that dataset does the second, larger job: it prices which pour conditions actually produce sub-36 surfaces, which lets the operator cut premiums for disciplined installers and refuse the reckless ones, and it becomes the evidence pack the installer uses to win the dispute (documented conditions at pour beat a homeowner's assertion). Payout capital is capped hard and structurally solo: a fixed daily rate, a 45-day ceiling, a per-installer annual aggregate, a 90-day qualifying period and a closed book of a few hundred installers — a self-funded contingency pool, not scale underwriting. Distribution is owned: the resin-bound installer world lives in a handful of Facebook groups and installer forums where per-batch cure knowledge is the currency, so publishing anonymised 'these pour conditions failed PTV' findings from the dataset is both the marketing and the product.

UK sole-trader and two-person resin-bound driveway and pathway installation firms turning over £80k–£250k a year — the ones trained by a resin supplier's one-day course rather than a surfacing apprenticeship — paying roughly £59/month for a bond paying £180/day for up to 45 days on a failed third-party pendulum test.

Resin-bound domestic surfacing has scaled from a niche to a mass driveway product across the UK in the last five years, pulling in thousands of newly-trained solo installers with no institutional memory of slip liability; simultaneously homeowners and their legal advisers have learned to reach for a pendulum slip test as the decisive objective measure in surfacing disputes, and UKSRG guidance on the 36 PTV threshold is now cited routinely in domestic claims. The result is a large cohort of solo operators newly exposed to a precise, measurable, expensive failure mode that did not have a name in their trade five years ago and that no existing product pays out on.
