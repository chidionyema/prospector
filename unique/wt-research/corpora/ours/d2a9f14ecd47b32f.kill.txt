SOC 2 evidence collection for AI startup CTOs.

A tool for startup CTOs whose SOC 2 auditor rejects screenshots of AI model governance, turning your model registry, eval runs and prompt changes into signed evidence the auditor accepts.

Here is the plain version. A software startup selling to US banks, hospitals or Fortune 500 buyers gets asked for a SOC 2 Type II report every single deal. The report is produced by a licensed CPA firm under AICPA rules. When the startup ships AI features, the auditor now asks a question nobody had a good answer for three years ago: prove that a change to a model or a prompt went through the same change-management control as a code deploy.

The founder-operator's actual day looks like this. The auditor's request list lands, and it asks for evidence of change approval, testing and rollback for every production change in the audit window. Their Git commits are covered by the existing tools. Their prompt edits, model version bumps, temperature changes and retrieval index rebuilds are not. So the CTO spends nights taking screenshots of their OpenAI or Anthropic console, pasting eval scores into a spreadsheet, and writing memos explaining why a prompt change is or is not a production change. The auditor comes back with follow-up questions, and it happens again next quarter for the bridge letter.

The tool sits in the startup's own pipeline. It watches the places model behaviour actually changes: the prompt files in the repo, the model IDs in config, the eval harness results, the feature flag that rolled a new model to production. Every one of those becomes a timestamped, immutable record with the approver, the test result before and after, and who rolled it back if it went wrong. At audit time the founder exports one file, and it is already in the shape the CPA firm's request list asks for.

This borrows something proven in a completely unrelated field. Pharmaceutical manufacturers have run batch records for decades under FDA rules: every change to a process is captured at the moment it happens, signed, and never edited afterwards, so the inspector reads the record rather than interviewing the operator. Nobody has applied that pattern to AI model changes in a software company, because the industry treats prompts as content rather than as controlled process steps.

Why it holds. Once this is wired into the startup's CI pipeline and it has produced the evidence for one clean audit, ripping it out means losing the historical record that the NEXT audit window compares against. The auditor asks for the full period. A startup that switches vendors in month nine has a gap it has to explain. That is a genuine cost of leaving, and it grows every quarter the tool runs.

The operator side is small. One or two engineers build the collectors for the six or seven places model changes happen. Selling is done by the founder in the same rooms where compliance automation vendors already sell, and by partnering with the CPA firms who audit these startups and are tired of chasing screenshots.

The technical co-founder or first head of engineering at a US software startup of roughly 15 to 80 people that has AI features in production and is going through SOC 2 Type II because enterprise buyers demand it. These are companies with real revenue and a security questionnaire blocking a deal. They already pay $10,000 to $50,000 a year to a compliance automation vendor and $20,000 to $60,000 to the CPA firm doing the audit, so the budget line exists and the buying decision is one person's.

The AICPA published its SOC 2 criteria update and its guidance on auditing systems that use AI, which pushed model change management from a nice-to-have into something auditors actively test. Separately the National Institute of Standards and Technology published its AI Risk Management Framework, and enterprise buyers now paste its control language directly into vendor security questionnaires. The result is that a startup founder gets asked to evidence something their existing compliance tooling does not collect, and they find out four weeks before the audit fieldwork starts.
