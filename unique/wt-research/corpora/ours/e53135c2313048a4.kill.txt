WattForge — GPU racks for developers, powered by wind farms paid to switch off.

A small UK rack room wired straight into a curtailed wind farm, renting cut-price GPU space to solo AI developers who need cheap power to train and run their own models.

WattForge builds a small rack room next to a wind farm in Scotland that the electricity system operator regularly tells to switch off, because the cables carrying power south are too full. Instead of that electricity going to waste, WattForge draws it straight from the turbine through a private wire, before it ever reaches the public grid. It fills the room with GPU servers and rents rack space and whole machines to solo AI developers and small teams who want to train or run their own models without paying cloud prices.

This only works because of a very British problem. Great Britain connects wind farms faster than it builds the cables to move their power south, so the system operator pays wind farms not to generate on windy days — a cost that has been running into the hundreds of millions of pounds a year and is forecast to keep climbing through the 2030s as more wind connects ahead of new transmission lines. That gap between what a wind farm can generate and what the cables can carry is a stranded, site-specific surplus of power. A rack room parked right next to the turbine can buy that power directly, at a price no data centre paying normal grid rates can match.

The lasting edge here is the wire, not the software. A GPU colocation business can be copied in a weekend. A private-wire connection into one specific curtailed wind farm, with the land lease, the network operator's sign-off and the turbine owner's agreement, cannot — each site is a one-off deal that takes months to arrange, and there are only so many curtailed wind farms with spare land next to a substation. Once a developer points their training jobs at WattForge's scheduling API — which reads the grid operator's own public curtailment and balancing data to predict the cheapest windows and bursts jobs into them — moving that workflow to a normal cloud means giving up a price curve nobody else can offer.

Solo AI and machine-learning developers, indie hackers and small startups who currently rent GPU instances from AWS, RunPod or Lambda Labs to fine-tune or run open-weight language models, and who want meaningfully cheaper compute for long training runs that don't need to sit next to their users.

Britain's wind curtailment bill has become a regular story since 2023, with the grid operator paying wind farms not to generate on windy days because the cables south are full, and both the grid operator and the regulator have flagged the cost rising through this decade as more wind connects ahead of new transmission lines. At the same time, a growing wave of developers want to fine-tune and self-host their own models and are actively shopping around for cheaper GPU hours.
