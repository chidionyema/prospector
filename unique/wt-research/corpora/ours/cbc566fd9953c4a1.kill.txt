GrantClock — the tool that turns a council's own missed deadlines into a paid Disabled Facilities Grant.

A tool for family carers waiting on home adaptations that logs every council delay against the legal deadline and produces the complaint that forces payment.

A carer applies to their council for a Disabled Facilities Grant so a parent or disabled child can get a stairlift, a level-access shower or a ramp. The Housing Grants, Construction and Regeneration Act 1996 says the council must decide within six months of a valid application, and must pay within twelve months of the application date. In practice waits of 18 months to two years are common, and the carer has no idea the clock is even running.

This is a simple tool the carer uses on their phone. It asks when the occupational therapy referral went in, when the assessment happened, when the application was accepted as valid, and what the council has said since. Every time the carer gets a letter, an email or a phone call, they photograph or paste it into the tool. The tool timestamps it and maps it against the statutory clock.

The output is what the carer actually needs. First, a dated timeline showing exactly which deadline the council has breached, quoting the section of the 1996 Act. Second, a stage-one complaint letter written in the language the Local Government and Social Care Ombudsman uses in its own published decisions. Third, when the council has had twelve weeks and not resolved it, the completed Ombudsman referral.

This takes the way tenants use rent-repayment timelines against landlords, and points it at the council. It works because the value grows as the council gets slower and more stretched, not less. The council cannot build this. It is the party being timed. A council can publish its grant guidance and a free application portal, and many do, but it structurally cannot ship a product whose purpose is to document its own breach and hand the evidence to the body that fines it. The Ombudsman itself cannot build it either, because it must stay impartial and can only look at a complaint once the council's own process has been exhausted — it cannot take the carer's side in getting there.

The hard part is the evidence base. Every Ombudsman decision on Disabled Facilities Grants is published, and each one names the council, the delay length, the fault found and the remedy paid — typically £250 to £500 a month for delay, plus the adaptation itself. Coding these into the tool means it can tell a carer in Birmingham what Birmingham has already been found at fault for, and what remedy that council has already agreed to pay. That library only gets more valuable with each new decision, and it is built by the operator's own reading, not scraped from anything a rival can buy.

Carers keep the tool through the whole case, which for a grant runs a year or more, and then reuse it for the second adaptation. The record of their case lives in it. Moving out means retyping a year of dated evidence.

Family carers of disabled children and disabled or older adults who have applied for a Disabled Facilities Grant in England or Wales and are stuck waiting. Around 50,000 of these grants are completed a year, and the waiting population is larger. Price it at £39 for the case, or £9 a month while the case is live. Parent carer forums, Contact, Scope's helpline and the SEN and disability groups on Facebook are where these people already talk to each other daily, and they share what worked.

The Disabled Facilities Grant maximum in England rose to £30,000 in December 2024, the first increase since 2008, and the government added funding for 2025-26. More money has pulled in more applicants without adding occupational therapists or council surveyors, so the queues have lengthened. The Local Government and Social Care Ombudsman has been publicly critical of grant delays and upholds a high share of the complaints it receives about them, which means a well-evidenced complaint now has a strong track record behind it.

The passages show carers of disabled people are already being advised to use council complaints procedures specifically about the delays they are experiencing, and to escalate to the ombudsman, with template complaint letters and support for filling in ombudsman forms being offered because people struggle with them [bfc73265aac40dab, 2ab05de848bd5838, ba1633e29fb98c1b]. That is people actively seeking help to complain about exactly this delay problem today, though the passages never say anyone pays money for such help.

The passages describe this exact payer group — unpaid family carers in the UK — as financially squeezed: 1.2 million are in poverty, 1 in 6 are in debt because of caring (rising to 2 in 5 in some groups), tens of thousands are being pursued over benefit overpayments, and many are excluded from Carer's Allowance altogether [325a2cf7d276c429, 9be9153d6391ba2f, e156d8f51960cfe8, ea77560898228501]. Nothing here shows these carers with spare money or any history of paying for a service, so the evidence cuts against their ability to fund even a £39 case fee rather than supporting it.
