Catchment Trade — finds UK growers a transferable water abstraction licence.

It helps commercial growers secure lawful water by finding suitable unused abstraction licences and handling the transfer with the Environment Agency.

A farm director facing a reduced or restricted abstraction licence can buy access from another licence holder in the same catchment, subject to Environment Agency approval. Catchment Trade finds suitable sellers, tests whether the licence can legally move, and brokers the sale through a rural solicitor.

The service combines the Environment Agency's public abstraction-licence records with hydrology, seasonal demand, catchment restrictions, previous transfer outcomes and completed prices. That produces a practical answer to a question the public register cannot answer: which available licence is actually worth pursuing for this field, crop and season?

The buyer pays a success fee when a transfer completes. The seller receives a share of the sale price, while the platform automates matching, document collection, eligibility screening and solicitor instructions.

This is most valuable when regulation tightens. The Environment Agency can restrict abstraction, refuse applications and impose drought measures under the Water Resources Act 1991. It cannot recommend a commercial counterparty or negotiate a price because it must remain the licensing authority.

The business starts with high-value horticulture in stressed catchments. It can grow through agricultural land agents, irrigation installers, rural solicitors and crop advisers. Every completed transaction adds private evidence about workable transfers, prices and approval outcomes, improving future matching and making the service harder to replace.

The payer is the 35-to-55-year-old owner-manager or finance director of a UK horticulture business growing salad crops, berries, flowers or glasshouse produce. They have expensive land, equipment finance and household commitments, but losing a crop or drilling a new borehole can create an immediate cash crisis.

Climate pressure and tighter catchment management are making reliable abstraction harder to secure. The Environment Agency's water-resources work increasingly favours sustainable abstraction and can restrict existing licences where rivers or aquifers are under pressure. The Water Resources Act 1991 provides the legal framework for abstraction licensing, while licence transfers still require case-by-case approval rather than a simple open-market purchase.
