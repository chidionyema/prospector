Export screen kits for AI startups shipping abroad.

A fixed-fee kit that classifies your AI model for export, screens your foreign buyer against US sanctions lists, and tells you in 48 hours whether you need a license.

When an early-stage AI startup closes a foreign customer, the founder discovers that shipping model weights, an API, or hosted inference to a buyer in Frankfurt, Dubai, or Singapore requires a formal export classification under the Export Administration Regulations (EAR, 15 CFR Parts 730-774). Most founders have never classified their model, and the procurement question on the buyer's purchase order is whether they have an Export Control Classification Number on file. Both answers are bad: ignore the rule and risk civil penalties of several hundred thousand dollars per violation or twice the value of the transaction (15 CFR § 764.3), or pay a trade compliance attorney $5,000 to $15,000 for a two-week memo that often comes back saying 'EAR99, no license required.'

This borrows the way a licensed US customs broker classifies every parcel against the Harmonized Tariff Schedule, applying the same intake-to-classification-to-determination workflow to AI software shipments. The buyer answers roughly 30 specific questions about their model (parameter count, training data sources, fine-tuning status, deployment method), their buyer (entity name, country, declared end-use), and their delivery mechanism (weights download, hosted API, on-prem appliance). The kit produces a four-document package in 48 hours: an ECCN classification memo citing the specific entry from 15 CFR Part 774 Supplement No. 5 (the Bureau of Industry and Security's January 15, 2025 interim final rule at 90 FR 4544 added AI-specific entries in Category 4 covering advanced AI training systems and AI model weights); an OFAC SDN screening report against the daily-updated sanctions list (31 CFR Part 501, Appendix A); a license determination with the regulatory section cited (e.g., 15 CFR § 744.21 for Entity List, § 744.11 for sanctioned destinations, § 744.23 for end-use and end-user controls); and a redlined BIS license application template if a license is in fact required.

The kit holds because the BIS rules are genuinely uncertain and an export attorney is the only other party qualified to issue a binding classification, but the kit captures the routine 80 percent of cases (EAR99 determinations for non-frontier model weights, controlled-jurisdiction flags, sanctioned-entity hits) that don't justify a $10,000 attorney bill. The encoded decision tree maps specific AI configurations to specific ECCNs and stays current with Federal Register publications, so when BIS publishes a new rule every paying customer receives a reclassification notice within 48 hours. Pricing is $1,500 for the standard kit and $3,500 with a one-hour attorney review of an edge-case memo, plus a $400-per-year subscription for reclassification alerts and an annual export posture review.

What could sink it: a major BIS rule change that the kit's owner fails to track, which is mitigated by subscribing to Federal Register alerts and BIS notices plus a quarterly legal update from a retained trade attorney at roughly $500 per quarter; a customer misclassifying their model and getting caught, mitigated by a clear liability disclaimer, a $5 million E&O insurance referral bundled with each kit, and a documented intake process the customer signs off on; the AI diffusion framework being reinstated in a form that overrides the kit's classifications, mitigated by rebuilding the same intake form against the new framework so existing customers can be reclassified without losing their purchase.

AI startup founder-operators at $100k to $5M ARR who have just signed or are about to sign their first international customer contract, typically with buyers in the EU, UK, Canada, Australia, Singapore, Japan, Korea, Israel, the UAE, or Latin America. The founder is wearing the trade compliance hat because no five-person startup has an in-house export attorney. A typical buyer has one signed contract in hand, one more in negotiation, and three months of anxiety about whether their existing foreign customers were correctly classified under the new AI-specific ECCNs.

The Bureau of Industry and Security published an interim final rule on January 15, 2025 (90 FR 4544) creating new Export Control Classification Numbers specifically for AI models in Category 4 of the Commerce Control List, covering advanced AI training systems and AI model weights. The Biden-era AI Diffusion Rule was rescinded in May 2025 after industry litigation in the US District Court for the Northern District of California. Most AI startups founded between 2023 and 2024 have never classified their products under the EAR and have been operating without any export classification at all. They are now exposed to enforcement retroactively, and any new foreign contract triggers the question of whether the model weights they are about to send across a border require a license.
