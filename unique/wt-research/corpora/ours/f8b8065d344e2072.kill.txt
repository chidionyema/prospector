DigitalVault Store — the walk-in shop that builds your digital legacy plan so your family isn’t locked out when you’re gone.

A chain of walk-in shops in retirement areas that helps older adults inventory their online accounts, set up legacy contacts, and store a secure plan for a fixed fee, just like pre-planning a funeral but for your digital life.

The funeral industry long ago proved that people will pay for ‘pre-need’ peace of mind — making decisions now so their family never has to. DigitalVault Store transplants that model to the rapidly growing world of online assets. Older Americans in states like Florida and Arizona hold bank accounts, brokerage logins, photo libraries, social media profiles, airline miles, and sometimes cryptocurrency. Yet when they pass or become incapacitated, their families face a digital scavenger hunt with no roadmap, often hitting brick walls behind privacy terms. A friendly storefront in a retirement community — staffed by trained, non-technical ‘digital guides’ — walks a person through a structured session. The output is a printed inventory, a set of legacy contacts configured on major platforms (using the customer’s own credentials, with the guide only pointing and explaining), and an encrypted digital copy stored in the shop’s vault. The service is a fixed fee of $199 for the initial plan, with an optional $49 annual subscription that includes an annual check-in call and a small safe-deposit-style record. The model scales by standardizing the session flow and training guides with a script; storefronts open in ZIP codes dense with retirees, like Sun City Center, FL or Green Valley, AZ. The analogical anchor is the pre-need funeral counselor who asks ‘what would you want your family to know?’ — but here the subject is a smartphone’s password manager, not a casket.

Older adults with active digital lives (banking, email, social media, photo storage) who live in retirement communities; many are themselves the payer. Adult children also buy the plan as a gift for aging parents who resist tech help. The fee is out-of-pocket and priced like a financial planning session, not a subscription to a software platform.

The Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act (RUFADAA) has been adopted by 47 states, giving fiduciaries a legal path to access digital accounts — but only if the owner has left clear instructions. Stories of families locked out of a parent’s Bitcoin wallet or unable to close a deceased spouse’s Facebook account are common in local news. At the same time, older adults are increasingly comfortable visiting established local storefronts but still wary of doing this type of planning online by themselves. No trusted, in-person chain exists to meet this specific demand, while estate planning attorneys rarely offer digital asset inventories as a standalone service.
