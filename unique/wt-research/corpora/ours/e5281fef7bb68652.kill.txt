KitSweep — find and buy reconditioned disability equipment from NHS and council surplus, so you pay a fraction of new.

A fixed-fee service that hunts down and buys you the exact NHS-decommissioned hoist or bed your relative needs, fully reconditioned and compliant.

Primary carers supporting a relative with a disability or mobility need often face bills of £2,000 or more for essential equipment like a ceiling hoist or profiling bed. Yet every month NHS Trusts and local authorities decommission large volumes of identical equipment, selling it through online auction platforms such as Hilditch Group. For a time-poor carer, bidding at auction is a gamble: lots are sold as-is, decontamination records are patchy, and there is no comeback if a hoist fails a critical safety test after delivery.

KitSweep acts as the carer’s personal buyer. The carer submits a precise requirement – make, model, maximum dimensions, weight capacity. The broker then scans upcoming decommissioning auctions, physically inspects promising lots (or uses a trusted network of local agents), bids up to a pre-agreed limit, and arranges professional refurbishment, PAT testing and delivery. The carer pays the auction hammer price plus a fixed broker fee of £300. The result is a fully compliant, reconditioned aid that typically costs 60–80% less than an equivalent new item.

This borrows the way fine-art brokers source at auction for private clients who cannot visit the saleroom themselves, but applies it to heavily regulated medical equipment where the buyer cannot afford to get it wrong. The operator compounds a proprietary dataset of supplier disposal schedules, lot condition notes and refurbisher performance, making repeat requests faster each time. The structural form is a transaction broker: the value is captured by making an illiquid, opaque market accessible to a single motivated buyer, without the broker ever holding inventory.

A primary carer (often an adult child) who needs to purchase a specific piece of mobility or care equipment for an elderly or disabled relative, but cannot afford the new retail price and lacks the time or confidence to navigate NHS surplus auctions.

MHRA guidance updated in April 2024 tightens the requirements for selling on used medical devices, making it harder for NHS Trusts to sell directly to the public and increasing the risk for unassisted buyers. At the same time, the cost-of-living crisis means more families are turning to reconditioned equipment but cannot bridge the trust gap. Auctioneers are seeing rising volumes from Trusts under space pressure, but no one is serving the individual carer with a managed sourcing service.
